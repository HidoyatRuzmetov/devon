# Open-source foundations and forkable products

**TL;DR:** No single open-source project matches the "Jira power, Notion flexibility, zero training" brief out of the box — the honest path is **compose, not fork**: a thin custom app for the department's actual objects (people, projects, weekly-comment ritual, activities) sitting on top of 2-3 boring, battle-tested open-source services for the parts that are commodity (auth/identity, file storage, chat). Plane (AGPL, 58.9k★) is the best *reference* for a Jira-lite UI to study pixel-by-pixel, but forking it inherits a Django+React monolith tuned for software teams, not policy analysts. The one government-grade precedent that actually works at scale is Germany's **openDesk** (ZenDiS, Apache-2.0, Nextcloud+OpenProject+XWiki+Element, live at the Bundeswehr and the ICC since 2024-25) — it proves "compose sovereign open-source components under one brand" is a viable government strategy, not just build vs. buy. License choice matters concretely: AGPL-3.0 (Plane, Nextcloud, Vikunja, Leantime, Documenso, Teable, Postiz, Rallly, Formbricks) is safe and even attractive for a government fork since the "network copyleft" obligation to publish modifications only bites if you *distribute* the modified service to outside parties — running it internally for one ministry triggers no disclosure duty at all. Watch for open-core bait-and-switch and mid-flight relicensing (Directus moved from AGPL/BSL to a new "Sustainable Core License" that is source-available, not OSI-open) — pin the version and read the license file, not the marketing page, before committing.

## CRM / structured-record patterns worth studying even if we don't need a CRM

## Government-specific building blocks and precedents

- **openDesk** (Germany, ZenDiS — Center for Digital Sovereignty in Public Administration; Apache-2.0; launched v1.0 Oct 2024): a *composed* suite — Collabora Online (office docs), Nextcloud (files), Open-Xchange (mail/calendar), Element (chat), Jitsi (video), **OpenProject (project management)**, XWiki (wiki, replacing Confluence), Univention Nubus (identity), ClamAV/Dovecot/Postfix (mail security infra). This is the single most directly relevant precedent for our brief: a government built a "department operating system" by integrating existing best-of-breed open-source products under one identity/branding layer rather than building or fully forking any one of them. **2025-26 adoption trajectory is real, not just an announcement**: Bundeswehr signed a 7-year deal (Apr 2025) via BWI; German Federal Ministry of Health began a 7,000-user rollout (Jun 2025); the International Criminal Court switched from Microsoft Office to openDesk (Oct 2025); German social insurance agencies began trials (Jan 2026).
- **Nextcloud** government footprint is broad and well-documented: named references include Germany's ITZBund (federal IT services body) and BMWi, the French Interior Ministry and Ministry of Ecological Transition, city governments (Stuttgart, Lyon, Geneva), and Upper Austria's regional government — with explicit "keep our citizens' and employees' data in-country, independent of US/foreign vendors" framing that maps directly onto Uzbekistan's data-localization requirement.
- **OpenProject** has a genuinely deep public-sector customer list (see table above) — Berlin, Cologne, two federal ministries, a state parliament, plus universities and Deutsche Bahn — making it the most "government-procurement-safe" project-tracking engine of everything surveyed, at the cost of a heavier, more traditional (Rails/Angular, ticket-and-Gantt-centric) UX than Plane.
- **X-Road** (Estonia's RIA, 2001; MIT license since 2016; now governed jointly by Estonia+Finland via NIIS): a secure inter-agency **data exchange layer**, not a workforce/project tool — but directly relevant to Phase 2/3 of our roadmap ("connected workflows," "stable IDs across systems") if Uzbekistan ever needs to exchange records between ministries. Worth flagging to the product owner as a distinct, later-phase investment area, separate from the department tool itself.
- **GovStack**: an open technical-blueprint initiative promoting a "building blocks" architecture (reusable specs + verified building-block software + a marketplace) for government digital services, with an emerging "AI readiness guide." Directionally aligned with our multi-tenant ambition but is more focused on citizen-facing service delivery (registries, payments, ID) than internal operations tooling — relevant mainly as a naming/positioning reference ("we are building government building blocks") rather than a source of code to fork.
- **Digital Public Goods Alliance / DPG Registry**: certifies ~250 open-source tools against a "DPG Standard" (open license, does-no-harm, relevance to SDGs, etc.). We did not find a specific DPG-certified internal-operations/workforce tool directly matching our brief, but the **certification framework itself** is worth adopting as a lightweight internal checklist when picking any dependency (open license + platform independence + clear data ownership).
- **Tchap** (France, DINUM + ANSSI, launched 2019; fork of Element/Matrix with federation, audio, and video **deliberately stripped out** for a sovereign, simplified footprint): adoption was slow for years until a **July 2025 government mandate** forced rollout by September 2025 — by early 2026 reaching ~400,000 monthly agents. **Direct lesson for us**: sovereign/self-hosted chat tools do not achieve viral adoption on UX merit alone against WhatsApp/Telegram habits; France needed a top-down mandate to hit real usage, and even then Tchap suffered a June 2026 attempted cyberattack on its infrastructure. This is a caution against assuming "government-grade and secure" will itself drive the "goes viral" ambition — the reference-site brief's own goal (voluntary, UX-driven adoption) is harder than what even a national mandate achieved for Tchap.

## Negative / contrarian evidence (what to worry about)

1. **License drift is real and recent, not theoretical.** Directus moved from a fully open license toward the source-available "Monospace Sustainable Core License," and Anytype ships under a bespoke "Any Source Available License" while still marketing itself with open-source language — both show that a "★★★" GitHub star count is not proof of a durable, forkable open license. Pin the exact commit/tag and license file you build on, and budget for the possibility that the vendor changes terms later (this is precisely why AGPL/MIT/Apache from a foundation or a company with an already-proven Community Edition, like OpenProject or Nextcloud, is lower-risk than a young VC-backed open-core startup).
2. **Open-core "self-host tax."** Multiple products gate meaningful functionality behind paid Enterprise tiers even when "self-hosted": Odoo Community is thin compared to Enterprise; Cal.com's limitations spawned an entire de-commercialized community fork ("Cal.diy") specifically because self-hosters felt short-changed; Novu, Twenty, Formbricks, Teable, Baserow all reserve some features for paid/enterprise editions. Expect to hit a paywall or a missing feature in any of these within the first few months of real use.
3. **Maintenance risk in the "exciting new tool" category.** Focalboard is officially unmaintained as a standalone project (folded into a Mattermost plugin); Huly's own hosted product is being shut down mid-2026 even as the open-source repo stays active — a sign the commercial entity behind an AGPL project can fail even while the code survives. Government procurement should weight "boring and funded" (OpenProject, Nextcloud, Odoo — all with clear multi-year commercial backing) over "exciting and fast-moving" for anything load-bearing.
4. **Sovereign/self-hosted tools do not win adoption on their own.** Tchap needed a ministerial mandate to reach real usage after six years of slow uptake, and even a mandated rollout can suffer security incidents. If we want organic, "it goes viral" adoption, the UX bar has to beat Telegram/Excel/WhatsApp habits directly — regulatory backing is not a substitute for genuinely better UX.
5. **Framework mismatch risk.** Several strong workspace tools (AppFlowy: Flutter/Rust; Anytype: Go/Electron/P2P local-first) use stacks that are excellent for their product but would fragment a small team's skill set if forked wholesale, and Anytype's local-first/P2P architecture is actively hostile to a shared, centrally-administered, audited department system of record.
6. **Community-health signals matter more than star count.** Outline's maintainers publicly reject low-effort AI-generated PRs; Documenso no longer accepts external community PRs at all. Both are reasonable maintainer decisions, but they mean "we'll just upstream our patches" is not a realistic plan for either project — any fork commitment there is closer to a hard fork than a collaborative one.

## Build-from-scratch vs. fork vs. compose

- **Fork one big product (e.g., Plane or Huly) and reskin it**: fastest to a demo, but inherits a data model (issues/cycles/sprints, or Notion-style all-in-one blocks) built for software teams or maximalist scope, not "people + projects + weekly comment + activities + org chart" for a policy department. AGPL is friendly for internal-only forks (no distribution trigger), but customizing a Django+React or Svelte+MongoDB monolith to feel "zero-training" for a civil servant is a large, continuous UX-stripping effort against the grain of the original product's audience.
- **Build fully from scratch**: maximum control over the exact "five-second readability" UX described in the reference audit, and avoids all license/dependency risk — but forfeits years of hardening on the commodity parts (auth, permissions, file storage, notification delivery, real-time collaboration) that open-source projects have already solved.
- **Compose (recommended)**: build the thin, opinionated app that *is* the product experience (Overview/People/Organization/Projects/Activities exactly as the reference audit describes, in our own stack, with the Friday-ritual and auto-escalation logic as first-class product features, not a plugin bolted onto someone else's issue tracker) — and reach for open-source **infrastructure**, not open-source **applications**, underneath it: Postgres, an auth/identity layer, S3-compatible object storage, and a notification/workflow engine pattern borrowed from Novu/Postiz's Temporal-based approach. This mirrors exactly what openDesk did at the suite level (compose Nextcloud + OpenProject + XWiki + Element under one identity), just at the scale of a single custom application instead of eight federated ones.

## What this means for us

1. **[ADOPT]** Study Plane's UI/IA in detail (command palette, inline doc AI, cycles/views) as the interaction reference for "Jira power, no Jira learning curve" — do not fork its codebase, clone its *patterns*.
2. **[ADOPT]** Use AGPL-3.0 (or MIT/Apache) open-source infrastructure pieces — Postgres, an object-storage layer, a notification engine — rather than any single all-in-one application; AGPL's network-copyleft only triggers on external distribution, which an internal government tool never does, so treat AGPL as functionally "free" for our use case.
3. **[ADOPT]** Route Telegram-based notifications/escalations through a small self-built worker calling the Telegram Bot API directly, informed by Novu's provider-abstraction pattern and Postiz's Temporal-based scheduling — do not deploy Mattermost/Rocket.Chat/Zulip as a parallel chat system Uzbek civil servants won't use over Telegram.
4. **[ADOPT]** Borrow Leantime's explicit design philosophy ("designed with ADHD/dyslexia/autism in mind," built for people who never wanted to learn a PM tool) as the north star for our own onboarding-free UX bar, even though we won't fork its PHP codebase.
5. **[ADAPT]** Treat OpenProject as the "if we ever need real Gantt/portfolio/PPM depth" fallback or integration target (it has the deepest government reference list of anything surveyed) rather than the primary UI — its Rails/Angular interface is heavier than the "five-second readability" bar the reference audit sets.
6. **[ADAPT]** Consider Documenso (AGPL, self-hostable e-signature) for Phase 2's "approval history" roadmap item instead of building e-signature from scratch — but budget for the fact that it no longer takes outside PRs, so any gap becomes our own fork to maintain.
7. **[ADAPT]** Consider Rallly's no-signup polling pattern directly for the Activities module's RSVP flow — it is a small, self-contained AGPL tool that solves exactly the "find a date, no accounts" problem the reference site's Activities section implies.
8. **[AVOID]** Do not fork Directus, Anytype, or NocoDB as core infrastructure expecting a stable free tier long-term — all three ship under source-available (not OSI-open) licenses that have already drifted once and can be revenue-gated later; if used at all, use only their free tier for genuinely non-critical, replaceable functions.
9. **[AVOID]** Do not adopt Focalboard as a dependency — it is officially unmaintained as a standalone project; if its Kanban UI is appealing, study it for ideas only.
10. **[AVOID]** Do not assume deploying a "sovereign" self-hosted chat/collaboration suite will itself drive adoption — Tchap needed a government mandate after six years of low uptake even in France; our "goes viral" ambition has to be earned through UX quality on top of infrastructure we control, not from the infrastructure choice alone.
11. **[AVOID]** Do not build the department's core people/project/activity data model inside a generic no-code database tool (NocoDB/Baserow/Teable) even though it's tempting for speed — the weekly-comment ritual, auto-escalation, and privacy-tiered fields described in the reference audit are exactly the kind of domain logic that outgrows a spreadsheet-shaped tool within two quarters.
12. **[ADAPT]** Watch openDesk's own architecture (Nextcloud for files, OpenProject for projects, XWiki for docs, Element for chat, one identity layer via Univention Nubus) as the template for how we *federate* rather than reinvent once the platform needs to scale to other ministries — multi-tenant identity/SSO is the one piece worth building on proven open-source (Keycloak-class) infrastructure from day one rather than rolling our own.
13. **[ADOPT]** Treat the DPG Standard's checklist (open license, platform-independent, clear data ownership, does-no-harm) as our own lightweight due-diligence gate before adopting any third-party open-source dependency, given how often licenses drift in this space.
14. **[AVOID]** Do not pick a stack (Flutter/Rust for AppFlowy, Go/Electron/P2P for Anytype, Ruby/Angular for OpenProject) purely because a reference tool uses it — choose the stack a small in-house team can hire for and maintain in Uzbekistan over a 5-10 year horizon; none of the surveyed tools' stacks should override that constraint.
15. **[ADAPT]** For HR-adjacent needs (leave, payroll-lite, timesheets) later in the roadmap, Frappe HR (GPL-3.0, actively developed, Docker-deployable) is a more current and better-maintained option than OrangeHRM (GPL-3.0 but visibly lower current GitHub activity) if a bolt-on HR module is ever needed rather than building one.

## Open questions

- Exact current GitHub star/commit-frequency numbers should be re-checked at decision time — this survey is a September 2026 snapshot and several of these projects (Huly especially, given its SaaS shutdown) may look different within months.
- We could not verify any confirmed Central Asian or CIS-region government deployment of any tool surveyed (Plane, OpenProject, Nextcloud, openDesk, etc.) — all named government adopters are EU/German/French/US. The product owner should decide how much weight to give precedents from a very different regulatory and procurement environment.
- Odoo's actual Community-vs-Enterprise feature gap (which HR/Project features are Enterprise-only in the current version) needs a hands-on trial, not just the license-page text, before ruling it in or out.
- Whether Uzbekistan's data-localization law would accept a Directus/NocoDB/Teable-style "self-hosted but vendor-controlled-update-channel" tool, versus requiring a codebase the government can audit and freeze indefinitely (favoring AGPL/MIT/Apache over source-available licenses) — this is a legal/policy question, not a technical one, and should be confirmed with counsel before any procurement commitment.
- Whether GovStack or the Digital Public Goods Registry has any building block specifically for internal workforce/project management (as opposed to citizen-facing registries/ID/payments) — this session's fetches found the initiatives' framing but not a concrete matching building block; worth a dedicated follow-up search once web-search quota resets.
- Cal.com's precise current license terms (this session's fetch of the marketing site did not surface exact license text) and the full rationale behind the community "Cal.diy" fork should be read directly from both projects' LICENSE files before any scheduling-tool decision.
- Whether Telegram's Bot API alone (vs. a self-hosted chat layer) satisfies any classified/restricted-channel requirement the ministry may have for sensitive HR or security-adjacent communications — flagged by Tchap's rationale for stripping federation/video from Element, which may or may not apply to our threat model.

### 1. Payload CMS — now evaluated

| **Payload CMS** | MIT | Next.js-native (React), Node.js/TypeScript, Postgres or MongoDB, GraphQL + REST | 44.6k★, 15.8k+ commits | Installs directly inside an existing `/app` Next.js folder, or standalone; Docker-friendly | Markets itself as "the open-source, fullstack Next.js framework" — a headless CMS that doubles as a general application backend |

**[ADAPT]** Do not adopt Payload as the platform itself (it is a CMS-shaped tool, not a project-tracker, and its admin-UI generation is optimized for content editors, not the Overview/Organization/roadmap-style views the reference audit calls for). ### 2. Identity / SSO / IAM layer — dedicated evaluation

The original report's recommendation #12 named this "the one piece worth building on proven open-source infrastructure from day one" but never evaluated a specific option. Four real candidates, fetched directly:

| Product | License | Stack | Stars (Sep 2026) | Shape | Multi-tenancy | Security signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Keycloak** | Apache-2.0 | Java/Quarkus | 36.6k★, 32k+ commits | Full IAM: admin console, login themes, SAML+OIDC+LDAP, "realms" | Realm-per-tenant is the standard multi-tenant pattern, widely deployed this way | CNCF-graduated, OpenSSF Best Practices badge + Scorecard; Red Hat-backed (also ships as Red Hat build of Keycloak / former RH-SSO) |
| **Authentik** | MIT core + proprietary Enterprise tier | Python/Django + Go (outpost) + Rust, TS frontend | 25.4k★, ~24k commits | Full IAM with a more modern admin UI than Keycloak's; "flows" (visual auth pipelines) as the signature feature | Supported but less battle-tested at government scale than Keycloak's realm model | Active CI/code-coverage; open-core split means some enterprise SSO connectors are paywalled |
| **Zitadel** | AGPL-3.0 with Apache-2.0/MIT carve-outs for specific directories | Go backend, Postgres ≥14, TS frontend | 14.9k★, 4.6k+ commits | Full IAM, cloud-native, OIDC-certified | **Native, first-class**: explicit three-level hierarchy (Identity System → Organizations → Projects) built for exactly the "one instance, many tenants/ministries" shape we need | No open-core split at all — monetizes via Zitadel Cloud SaaS, not feature-gating self-hosters, which is a cleaner posture than Authentik's |
| **Ory Kratos** | Apache-2.0 | Go, Postgres/MySQL/CockroachDB | 13.9k★ | **Headless only** — no bundled admin UI or login pages; you build the UI against its API, and need the separate **Ory Hydra** project bolted on for OAuth2/OIDC token issuance | Build-your-own; maximum flexibility, maximum integration work | Apache-2.0, no enterprise/community split in the core, but SCIM/SAML/orgs are Ory Enterprise-licensed add-ons |

**Verdict:** Zitadel's native multi-tenant hierarchy is the best conceptual fit for "one platform, many ministries" and its licensing has no open-core trap, but it is the youngest project here (14.9k★, 4.6k commits vs. Keycloak's 32k+) and has the smallest government-adoption paper trail of the four. Keycloak is the "boring and funded" choice consistent with recommendation #3 in the negative-evidence section elsewhere in this report (CNCF governance, OpenSSF certification, 10+ years in production at large enterprises and governments worldwide) — the realm-per-tenant model is a well-worn pattern other multi-tenant SaaS-on-Keycloak deployments already use, even though its admin UI and Java/Quarkus operational footprint are heavier than Zitadel's. Ory Kratos should be ruled out for us specifically: its headless, assemble-it-yourself nature (plus a mandatory second project, Hydra, for OIDC) is exactly the kind of "continuous integration effort" the report's own fork-vs-build calculus warns against for a small team. Authentik sits in between — nicer UX than Keycloak out of the box, but younger governance and an open-core split to watch.

**[ADOPT]** Keycloak (Apache-2.0) as the identity/SSO layer for v1 — CNCF governance and OpenSSF certification are exactly the "boring and funded" signal this report elsewhere argues for, and realm-per-tenant is a proven pattern for the multi-ministry ambition.
**[ADAPT]** Re-evaluate Zitadel at the point multi-tenancy actually needs to scale past a handful of ministries — its purpose-built tenant hierarchy and cleaner (non-open-core) licensing may outweigh Keycloak's maturity edge once the org-hierarchy modeling becomes the harder problem than raw stability.
**[AVOID]** Do not build on Ory Kratos/Hydra for v1 — headless-only architecture means shipping our own login UI and wiring two separate Ory projects together, which is meaningfully more integration work than Keycloak or Zitadel for a team that needs to ship fast.

### 4. Security posture / CVE history — investigated, not punted

Direct reads of each project's GitHub Security Advisories page (as of Sep 2026):

- **Keycloak**: 10 recent advisories visible (6 High, 4 Moderate), most recently 7 advisories published **2026-08-06** and 3 more on **2026-06-26** — including an authentication-bypass-via-JWT-algorithm-confusion issue, an authorization bypass, role forgery in dynamic client registration, and a SAML broker flaw. At least 9 pages of advisories exist in total (i.e., a long history), consistent with an actively-scrutinized, widely-deployed IAM product — CNCF governance and OpenSSF certification mean these get triaged and patched through a formal process, not silence.
- **OpenProject**: 10 recent advisories visible, including **2 Critical** — "cache store poisoning leads to Remote Code Execution" and an IDOR affecting project storage settings — plus 2 High (cleartext token storage, CSRF on user administration) and 4 Moderate, published **2026-06-08** (8 advisories) and **2026-07-08** (2 advisories). The Critical RCE is a genuine go/no-go-relevant finding: it means OpenProject has had a remotely-exploitable critical vulnerability within the last quarter, which argues for strict, fast patch-application discipline if adopted, not a "set and forget" install.
- **Nextcloud**: the GitHub Security Advisories tab shows **zero** published advisories — but this reflects Nextcloud's disclosure *channel* choice, not a clean vulnerability record: Nextcloud runs its security program through a dedicated HackerOne bug-bounty program (hackerone.com/nextcloud) and its own security-release notes rather than GitHub Security Advisories, so "0 GitHub advisories" should not be read as "0 CVEs" — it needs checking directly against HackerOne/CVE databases before being used as a comparison point, which this session's fetch of `nextcloud.com/security/` confirmed exists as a formal program (public disclosure after each fix ships, current supported branches 34.0/33.0/32.0) without surfacing a specific count.
- **Plane**: has a conventional email-based responsible-disclosure policy (security@plane.so, 3-business-day acknowledgment SLA) but no GitHub Security Advisories history was surfaced in this pass — too new/small a program to compare directly against Keycloak/OpenProject's public advisory volume.

**Read across candidates:** raw advisory *count* is a poor signal on its own — it mostly reflects how mature and public a project's disclosure process is, not how insecure the software is (Keycloak's 10-per-year cadence via a CNCF-grade process is arguably healthier than a project with zero advisories and no visible bug-bounty program). The one fact that should directly inform procurement: **OpenProject shipped a Critical remote-code-execution advisory in mid-2026** — not disqualifying, but it means whichever team operates OpenProject needs a subscribe-and-patch-within-days workflow (GitHub Security Advisories RSS/watch, or Crowdin-adjacent OpenProject security mailing list), not quarterly patching.

**[ADOPT]** Subscribe to GitHub Security Advisories (watch → Custom → Security alerts) for every composed-infrastructure repo actually deployed (Keycloak, OpenProject and/or Nextcloud, Zitadel if chosen) before go-live, and write a "patch within N business days of a High/Critical advisory" rule into the operations runbook now, informed by OpenProject's 2026 Critical RCE.
**[AVOID]** Do not treat Nextcloud's empty GitHub Security Advisories tab as evidence it is "more secure" than OpenProject or Keycloak — it reflects a different (HackerOne-based) disclosure channel, and that channel's actual CVE history should be pulled from the NVD/CVE database directly before any comparative security claim is made in a procurement document.

### 5. TCO / self-hosting sizing and cost

OpenProject publishes concrete, tiered hardware-sizing guidance that is a reasonable proxy for the whole "compose" architecture's likely footprint (its Rails/Postgres/Redis-adjacent stack is representative of the class of self-hosted service we'd be running two or three of):

| Users | CPU | RAM | Web workers |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≤200 | 4 cores (≥2GHz) | 4 GB | 2 workers × 4 threads |
| ~500 | 8 cores | 8 GB | 4 workers |
| ~1500 | 16 cores | 16 GB | 8 workers |

For today's ~23-person department, a **single 4-core/4GB VM comfortably covers OpenProject-class load with enormous headroom** — the ≤200-user tier is already ~9x current headcount. Practically, that means for the "compose" recommendation (custom app + Postgres + Keycloak + object storage), a realistic v1 self-hosted footprint is **2-4 modest VMs (4 vCPU / 8GB RAM class each)** — one for the custom app + Postgres, one for Keycloak, one for object storage/backups, optionally one for a reverse proxy/load balancer — well within a single on-prem rack or a modest gov-cloud tenancy, and cheap to over-provision given how far below OpenProject's own ≤200-user tier a 23-person department sits. Scaling to "other departments and ministries" (the multi-tenant ambition) should be planned against OpenProject's own ~500-user (8 core/8GB) and ~1500-user (16 core/16GB) tiers as rough per-service checkpoints, not a reason to over-build infrastructure now.

**[ADOPT]** Size v1 infrastructure at roughly 2-4 VMs in the 4 vCPU/8GB class (custom app+DB, identity, storage, proxy) using OpenProject's published ≤200-user tier as the sanity-check baseline — this is inexpensive enough that infra cost should not be a driver of the fork-vs-compose decision either way.
**[ADAPT]** Re-run this sizing exercise once the multi-tenant rollout to other ministries is scheduled, using OpenProject's ~500 and ~1500-user tiers as the next two checkpoints for when to add read replicas, load-balance web workers, or split Keycloak onto its own dedicated node.

### 7. Patch/upgrade cadence and backup/disaster-recovery

- **Nextcloud** publishes a concrete, checkable backup procedure: enable maintenance mode, then back up five components — the `config` folder, the `custom apps` folder, the `data` folder, the `theme` folder, and the database (with documented `mariadb-dump`/`mysqldump`/`pg_dump`/SQLite commands per backend) — before restoring from a documented restore procedure. This is a directly actionable DR checklist, not a hand-wave.
- **OpenProject**'s equivalent backup-documentation page could not be fetched this session (404), so its specific backup guidance should be pulled fresh at implementation time rather than assumed to mirror Nextcloud's; general Rails-app practice (dump Postgres, back up the attachments/files volume and any local config) almost certainly applies but was not independently confirmed here.
- **Patch cadence**, read across the security-advisory data gathered in section 4: Keycloak and OpenProject both ship advisories in **clustered monthly-to-bimonthly batches** (Keycloak: Jun and Aug 2026; OpenProject: Jun and Jul 2026), implying both projects release security patches on a roughly monthly rhythm rather than continuously — which is good news for a small ops team (a monthly "check advisories, apply patches" cadence is plannable) but bad news if a Critical (like OpenProject's RCE) lands between cycles and needs an out-of-band patch.
- **Composed-infrastructure DR implication**: because the "compose" recommendation stacks 2-4 independently-versioned services (custom app+Postgres, Keycloak, object storage), disaster recovery is not one backup job — it is at minimum: (1) Postgres logical dumps for both the app DB and Keycloak's own DB, (2) object-storage bucket replication/snapshotting, (3) Keycloak realm-config export (Keycloak supports full realm JSON export/import, making config portable independent of its DB), and (4) infrastructure-as-code (Docker Compose files, environment secrets in a vault) so the whole stack can be rebuilt from scratch, not just data-restored. None of the surveyed projects publish a single combined "compose stack DR" runbook — this has to be authored in-house, treating each component's own backup story (Nextcloud's is well-documented; Postgres's `pg_dump`/WAL archiving is industry-standard; Keycloak's realm export is well-documented separately) as building blocks.

**[ADOPT]** Adopt Nextcloud's five-component backup checklist as the template shape for our own custom app's backup runbook (config / custom-code / data / theme-or-branding / database) even if Nextcloud itself isn't used, since it maps cleanly onto any Postgres-backed web app.
**[ADOPT]** Build a monthly "check security advisories, apply patches" calendar reminder into the operations plan from day one, informed by the observed Keycloak/OpenProject bimonthly advisory rhythm — and a separate out-of-band emergency-patch procedure for Critical-rated advisories specifically (OpenProject's 2026 RCE is the concrete example to plan against).
**[ADOPT]** Treat Keycloak's realm-JSON export/import as the identity layer's DR mechanism, and write an explicit, tested "rebuild the whole compose stack from IaC + backups" runbook before go-live rather than only testing individual-component restores.
