# GovTech design patterns and how internal gov tools spread

**TL;DR:** The states that got public-sector digital right (UK, US-pre-2025, Estonia, Denmark, Finland, Singapore) all separated three layers — a *content/design system* (how it looks and reads), a *shared platform* (pay, notify, identity, interop) that departments plug into instead of rebuilding, and a *service standard/assessment gate* that keeps quality up without a big central team building everything. Adoption spread the way successful internal tools always spread — Slack, Asana, Jira all started as one team's tool that got good enough to hand to another team, then another, before anyone called it a "platform." Political reality is a live risk, not a footnote: the US model (18F/USDS) built exactly this kind of infrastructure and was shut down in weeks in 2025 for reasons that had nothing to do with the technology. National "everything app" benchmarks (Diia, Gosuslugi, eGov.kz) prove that a genuinely simple, life-event-organized front end can carry huge adoption, but their most instructive lessons are about single sign-on, once-only data, and blast-radius of centralization, not about their internal dashboards, which none of them publish. For Uzbekistan's data-localization constraint, the closest working precedent is Germany/EU's openDesk (self-hosted, EU-owned stack, explicit rejection of dependency on foreign clouds) rather than any SaaS design system.

## 1. Design systems as the unit of virality, not the visual layer

The three major public-sector design systems converge on the same architecture: a components/patterns library, a *content style guide* that is treated as equally important as the code, and a governance model that lets any team contribute back.

| | GOV.UK Design System (UK) | USWDS (US) | Singapore Government Design System |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maintainer | GDS (Government Digital Service) | GSA Technology Transformation Services (built with 18F, now TTS-only after March 2025) | GovTech Singapore |
| License | Open Government Licence v3.0, Crown copyright | Public domain / open source | Open source, "design once, use everywhere" |
| Scale claimed | Used across GOV.UK's ~300 services; content style guide is mandatory for anything published on GOV.UK | "Dozens of agencies and nearly 200 sites," incl. VA.gov, NASA, NIH Library, vote.gov | Framework-agnostic tokens (ships to React, Vue, Angular, Svelte, vanilla JS via Tailwind v4) |
| Version at time of writing | Continuously versioned via GitHub | 3.14.0 (Aug 2024; **stale flag** — ~2 years old as of Sept 2026 vantage point, re-verify current version before citing as a live reference implementation; see govuk-frontend's own GitHub releases confirming that GDS's system, by contrast, ships monthly — v6.5.0 as of Aug 2024, ongoing) | Actively iterating, no single frozen version publicized |
| What's actually in it | Styles (layout/type/colour), accessible components, *patterns* (multi-step forms, "check your answers," start pages) | 40+ components, design tokens, utility CSS, prebuilt templates, a "maturity model" for staged adoption | Component library + design tokens, cross-framework story |

Sources: [design-system.service.gov.uk](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/), [designsystem.digital.gov](https://designsystem.digital.gov/), [designsystem.tech.gov.sg](https://www.designsystem.tech.gov.sg/).

The load-bearing detail most teams miss: **GDS treats the design system as inseparable from the Service Manual and the Service Standard** — a components library without an accompanying set of *service assessment* criteria is decoration. The Service Manual covers eight things as one system: accessibility/assisted-digital, agile delivery, design practice, measuring success with data, service assessments, technology choices, team/hiring, and user research. A team can't ship a "GOV.UK-look" page that skips these — assessors check for it. ([gov.uk/service-manual](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual))

The specific, quotable content rule worth stealing outright is the **question-page / one-thing-per-page pattern**: "ask just one question per question page… every question page must include a back link, a page heading (question or statement), and a Continue button (labelled 'Continue,' not 'Next')." Progress indicators are explicitly discouraged by default — "test forms without progress indicators first" — because complex all-questions-at-once indicators "consume space, confuse users, and don't scale well on mobile." The one documented exception: **internal government services can group related questions on one page when user research shows staff need faster task-switching** — i.e., the "one thing per page" rule is a citizen-facing default, not dogma, and GDS itself says internal tools may reasonably break it. ([design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/question-pages/))

Singapore's version leans harder into a "write once, ship anywhere" technical story (framework-agnostic tokens compiled to Tailwind) rather than a content-first philosophy — evidence that a design system can spread on developer convenience alone, without GOV.UK's editorial rigor, if the org already has strong internal engineering platform teams. GovTech's public product taxonomy for agencies groups tools by function (accessibility testing via "Oobee," cybersecurity, data/AI, service delivery/case management, software stacks, knowledge/training via a Digital Academy) rather than by department — a useful signal that Singapore organizes shared tooling around *capability*, not org chart. ([tech.gov.sg/products-and-services](https://www.tech.gov.sg/products-and-services/))

## 2. Shared platforms beat shared components for actual cross-agency adoption

Design systems reduce inconsistency; **shared platforms are what actually gets adopted**, because they remove work rather than adding a style guide to follow. The clearest UK evidence:

| Platform | What it removes | Adoption evidence |
|---|---|---|
| GOV.UK Notify | Building your own email/SMS/letter sending + templates + delivery tracking | **1,754 organisations and 12,670 services** use it; free emails, 20,000 free texts/year, no procurement process required ([notifications.service.gov.uk](https://www.notifications.service.gov.uk/)) |
| GOV.UK Pay | PCI compliance, payment gateway integration, refunds | **1,500+ live services**, **70,000+ payments/day**, 99.99% uptime, used by HM Passport Office, MoJ, MoD, NHS, police forces, local councils ([payments.service.gov.uk](https://www.payments.service.gov.uk/)) |
| X-Road (Estonia/Finland) | Point-to-point integrations between every pair of agency systems | National interop backbone since 2001 (Estonia) and connected to Finland's Suomi.fi layer since Feb 2018; MIT-licensed, jointly governed by NIIS (Estonia+Finland, est. 2017) ([wikipedia.org/wiki/X-Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-Road)) |
| Suomi.fi (Finland) | Per-service authentication and per-agency messaging | Single sign-on across agencies, secure messaging, "authority management and delegation" as a first-class concept — a citizen can grant a proxy (accountant, family member) explicit rights per service ([suomi.fi](https://suomi.fi/)) |
| Denmark's NemID/MitID, NemKonto, NemLog-in | Duplicate identity/payment/login builds per agency | Positioned as compulsory shared components under a "fællesoffentlig digital arkitektur" (common public digital architecture) — adoption is closer to mandate than opt-in ([digst.dk](https://www.digst.dk/)) |

The pattern across all five: **the shared thing is a boring, narrow utility (send a message, take a payment, verify identity, exchange one field of data) that is *free or near-free at the point of use* and requires no procurement.** GOV.UK Notify's own pitch is explicit about this: "no monthly charge, no setup fee and no procurement process." That absence of friction is doing more adoption work than any design-system component library ever could. For a 23-person department trying to "go viral" internally, the lesson is not "build a beautiful component library" — it's "build the one thing (e.g., a notification/escalation service, a document-numbering service) that every other department would rather use than rebuild."

## 3. What "national superapp" benchmarks actually teach (and don't)

Diia (Ukraine), Gosuslugi (Russia) and eGov.kz (Kazakhstan) are the three most-cited "gov app done right" references in this region, but they are citizen-facing benchmarks, not internal-tool benchmarks — none publish how their own staff track internal work.

**Diia** launched Feb 2020 with digital driver's licence/car registration; by end of 2020 it had 6M+ app users and 50 portal services; it now covers 130+ services, added online marriage registration (Sept 2024, 435 couples in month one), and in March 2021 Ukraine became "the first country where digital identity documents are legally equivalent to physical ones." It made TIME's 200 Best Inventions of 2024. It also had a real breach: a May 2020 leak exposed 26 million driver's licence records, and it operates inside what Wikipedia's sourcing calls "Ukraine's weak data protection legal regime" — a genuine cautionary note on moving fast with citizen data. ([wikipedia.org/wiki/Diia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diia))

**Gosuslugi** (RU) launched Dec 2009, had 126M registered users by Dec 2020, and by 2023 averaged **11 million daily visitors** and delivered **340 million services** in the year — the scale argument for a single national portal design working at population scale. It also absorbed 50+ DDoS attacks >1TB capacity during the Feb 2022 invasion, showing that a single centralized portal is a single point of failure under geopolitical stress. ([wikipedia.org/wiki/Gosuslugi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gosuslugi)) We could not independently verify details of Russia's newer "GosTech" unified platform initiative or Russia's interagency data-exchange system (SMEV) — both are real and frequently referenced in Russian-language sources, but our fetch tools could not reach ru.wikipedia.org or Russian-language coverage in this session; treat any specifics on GosTech/SMEV as unverified (see Open Questions).

**eGov.kz** (Kazakhstan): we were unable to retrieve a working source this session (repeated 404s/timeouts on both the live site and Wikipedia); do not cite specific eGov.kz numbers without separate verification.

The actionable takeaway from Diia/Gosuslugi as *design* references (not infrastructure references) is organizing navigation around **life events / actual tasks** ("Family," "Health," "Business Creation") rather than agency org-chart categories — directly relevant to our brief's ambition to be understood by "a first-time civil servant... without training." A department ops tool should be organized around what a specialist is trying to do this week (report status, request leave, find a document) not around which sub-department owns the menu item.

## 4. How internal tools actually go viral — company case studies

This is the more directly transferable body of evidence, because our situation (one department's internal tool that should spread to other ministries) is structurally identical to "one team's internal tool that became the company's/industry's tool."

| Tool | Origin | Mechanism of spread |
|---|---|---|
| **Slack** | Internal IRC-based tooling built at Tiny Speck to coordinate development of a game, *Glitch*, that failed commercially (Oct 2012) | Stewart Butterfield repackaged the internal comms layer as a standalone product; public launch Aug 2013 got **8,000 signups in 24 hours**; freemium + shareable workspace URLs created bottom-up, team-by-team viral spread — "10,000 new daily active users/week" by Feb 2015, 135,000 paying customers across 60,000 teams 18 months post-launch ([wikipedia.org/wiki/Slack_(software)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slack_(software))) |
| **Asana** | Built as an internal tool called "Tasks" at Facebook by Dustin Moskovitz (co-founder) and Justin Rosenstein | Spun out in 2008, free beta Nov 2011, paid Apr 2012; grew via word-of-mouth/enterprise credibility from Facebook pedigree to 35,000 paying customers by Jan 2018 (Uber, eBay, IBM) ([wikipedia.org/wiki/Asana_(software)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asana_(software))) |
| **Jira** | Launched 2002 at Atlassian as a developer bug tracker; name is an internal joke ("Gojira"/Godzilla, referencing their prior tool Bugzilla) | Expanded beyond engineering once Atlassian opened a **plugin Marketplace in 2012**, letting third parties build portfolio/PM extensions — the ecosystem, not the core tool, is what let it colonize non-technical departments ([wikipedia.org/wiki/Jira_(software)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jira_(software))) |

Three mechanisms recur, and all three matter for us:
1. **The tool was genuinely used daily by its own builders before anyone else saw it** (dogfooding removes 90% of the "civil servant can't figure this out" risk before launch).
2. **Spread was peer-to-peer and bottom-up**, not top-down mandate — a team invited another team because it solved a felt problem, not because of a directive. This is the opposite of most gov IT rollouts (which mandate top-down) and is exactly the "goes viral" mechanism the brief wants.
3. **Zero/near-zero cost and zero procurement friction at the point where the second team tries it** (Slack's freemium tier, GOV.UK Notify's "no procurement process") — the single biggest predictor of whether tool #2 ever adopts tool #1's internal system.

## 5. Government-specific adoption mechanics — and how fast they can be destroyed

Government adoption differs from company adoption in one critical way: **a central digital-services unit (GDS, 18F/USDS, GovTech) has to earn the right to be used by every other agency, repeatedly, because agencies are not economically incentivized the way competing teams inside one company are.** The mechanisms that worked:

- **Service Standard + assessment gate** (UK): a team cannot ship a public service without passing an assessment against explicit criteria — this is a *quality floor*, not a style suggestion, and it is what keeps a design system from becoming optional decoration.
- **Communities of practice**: GDS explicitly describes spread happening through "communities of practice" connecting "digital people... from across government" — informal cross-agency peer networks, not org-chart mandates.
- **Procurement reform**: GDS deliberately shifted spend toward SMEs instead of large legacy suppliers — removing the economic reason agencies default to expensive bespoke builds. ([wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Digital_Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Digital_Service))
- **A dedicated cross-government delivery unit that dogfoods its own tools** (18F, founded March 19, 2014, named for its D.C. office address at 18th & F St; built USWDS, login.gov, cloud.gov, notify.gov). Peaked at 250+ staff (2018), had shrunk to ~90 by 2024, and **85% of departing staff in 2023 moved to other government-technology roles** — i.e., the org's influence kept spreading even as headcount shrank, because people, not just software, carried the practice into new agencies. ([wikipedia.org/wiki/18F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18F))

And the sharpest negative lesson in this entire research dimension: **18F was eliminated on March 1, 2025, at 1am, "under direction from the White House," with staff locked out of systems with no transition plan**, as part of the DOGE (Department of Government Efficiency) restructuring; Elon Musk had publicly called it "a far-left agency." USDS itself was renamed "U.S. DOGE Service" the same administration, lost dozens of staff in February 2025 and had 21 more resign rather than "compromise core government systems"; by November 2025 officials said DOGE "didn't exist" as a centralized body, having been absorbed piecemeal into departments. ([wikipedia.org/wiki/18F](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/18F), [wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Digital_Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Digital_Service)) **The infrastructure and the design system survive (USWDS, login.gov are still running), but the *organization that maintained and spread them* can be deleted in a single administrative action regardless of technical merit.** For our context: political sponsorship for this platform inside the ministry needs to be broader than one champion, and the artifact (code, docs, data) must be resilient/exportable even if the internal team that built it is reorganized.

## 6. Failure modes worth naming explicitly

| Failure | What happened | Cost/scale | Root cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| GOV.UK Verify | National ID-verification-as-a-service; 22 connected services at launch | £130M development cost, launched 4 years late (2016 vs. planned 2012), only 47% success rate by Oct 2018, down to 5 of 22 services by Oct 2022, killed April 2023 | Relied on a marketplace of private identity providers who progressively withdrew; didn't support businesses/intermediaries — designed for a narrower case than government actually needed ([wikipedia.org/wiki/GOV.UK_Verify](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GOV.UK_Verify)) |
| NHS National Programme for IT | Centralized, five-region, multi-contractor NHS records system | Budgeted £2.3B/3yr; National Audit Office estimate £12.4B/10yr, some estimates to £20B; called "one of the worst and most expensive contracting fiascos ever" by Parliament's Public Accounts Committee (2013) | Big-bang centralization, fragmented regional contracts, physician support collapsed from 70% to 41% (2005 survey) because clinicians weren't engaged; two of four prime contractors withdrew | 
| US healthcare.gov / USDS founding trigger | Federal exchange site broke at 2013 launch | Triggered creation of USDS (Aug 11, 2014) as a direct response | Classic large-vendor, big-bang, no-agile-iteration federal IT delivery model |
| Design-system-as-decoration | Not one incident, but a documented risk pattern across all three systems reviewed | — | A components library adopted for *look* without the accompanying content standards / service-assessment gate produces GOV.UK-styled pages that are still confusing — the visual system is necessary but not sufficient |

The consistent thread: **large, centralized, single-vendor, big-bang government IT fails; small teams building narrow, boring, iterated shared services (Notify, Pay, X-Road, login.gov) succeed.** This directly validates the reference prototype's own roadmap ordering (reliable records → connected workflows → decision intelligence) — start narrow and real, not comprehensive and speculative.

## 7. Data localization precedent: openDesk / ZenDiS (directly relevant to Uzbekistan)

Germany's ZenDiS (Center for Digital Sovereignty in Public Administration) built **openDesk**, a self-hostable Microsoft 365 alternative for public administration: Collabora Online (docs/sheets), Nextcloud (storage), Open-Xchange (mail/calendar), Element (chat), Jitsi (video), OpenProject (PM), XWiki. Version 1.0 shipped October 2024 after ~€45M investment; the German military (Bundeswehr) signed a 7-year contract in April 2025; the International Criminal Court announced a switch from Microsoft Office in October 2025 — driven specifically by concern over **US sanctions risk against ICC officials** (the US had sanctioned ICC personnel in prior years, and Microsoft is a US company subject to US export-control/sanctions law, meaning a foreign government could in principle order a productivity-suite vendor to cut off a sitting international court), not an abstract preference for "sovereignty" — a sharper and more concrete argument for data-localization than the vaguer framing usually given; German health/social-insurance agencies began trials January 2026. It runs on Kubernetes (5–6 servers), with explicit expert warnings **against** hosting it on AWS or Azure because that would defeat the sovereignty purpose. ([wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenDesk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenDesk))

This is the closest real precedent to Uzbekistan's constraint (citizen/employee personal data must stay in-country): a sovereignty-driven government chose **open-source self-hosted components stitched together**, not a single monolithic vendor product, and is willing to accept a multi-year, multi-agency rollout to get there. It also validates that this is *hard and slow even for a G7 government with a dedicated agency and tens of millions of euros* — worth calibrating expectations.

## 8. Instruction → executor → deadline → report (the "ispolnitelskaya distsiplina" pattern)

The brief specifically asks about this because it is central to how CIS/post-Soviet civil services (including Uzbekistan's) actually run day to day: a leader issues an **instruction/поручение** with a named executor and a deadline; a document-management system (СЭД/EDMS — e.g., "Дело," "Directum," or bespoke ministry systems) logs it, tracks status, and produces **execution-discipline (исполнительская дисциплина) reports** — dashboards of on-time vs. overdue instructions, escalated up the hierarchy, often by name. This is functionally the same primitive as the reference prototype's "Friday comment ritual + auto-escalation to management review queue" for projects — just applied to *any* instruction, not only projects. **We were not able to verify current specifics of Russia's GosTech platform or the SMEV interagency system this session** (network access to Russian-language sources was blocked), so treat exact architecture claims about those systems as unverified; the *pattern itself* (instruction/executor/deadline/report, with automatic escalation of overdue items) is well-established general knowledge of CIS public administration and matches this department's own existing "auto-escalation" idea almost exactly — it is worth treating as a first-class object (not just for projects, but for any instruction from the director or a sub-department head), because it is the one workflow every civil servant in this system will recognize on day one with zero training.

## What this means for us

1. **[ADOPT]** A written content style guide (plain language, one-thing-per-page as *default* for anything citizen- or leadership-facing) before a component library — GOV.UK proves the writing standard, not the CSS, is what keeps a tool usable at scale.
2. **[ADAPT]** "One thing per page" as a default for external/citizen-facing forms, but explicitly allow grouped fields on internal specialist screens — GOV.UK's own pattern guidance says internal services can break this rule when it speeds up task-switching; don't cargo-cult it into our internal ops screens.
3. **[ADOPT]** Build the "instruction → executor → deadline → report" object as a first-class, reusable primitive (not just for projects) with automatic escalation on missed deadlines — it is the single workflow every Uzbek civil servant already recognizes, and it's the reference prototype's own best idea generalized.
4. **[ADOPT]** Treat one narrow, boring, shared service (e.g., an internal notification/escalation engine, akin to GOV.UK Notify) as the actual viral unit — make it free, zero-setup, and usable by another sub-department or ministry in under 5 minutes, not the whole platform at once.
5. **[AVOID]** A single centralized, big-bang, all-departments-at-once rollout — GOV.UK Verify (£130M, 4 years late, 47% success) and NHS NPfIT (£12.4B+, called Parliament's worst fiasco) are the canonical failures of exactly this pattern; ship to one team, get it dogfooded, then expand.
6. **[ADOPT]** A lightweight internal "service assessment" checklist (does it have an owner, a weekly update ritual, a privacy tier, an escalation rule?) before a module counts as "done" — cheap version of GDS's Service Standard gate, appropriate for a 23-person team.
7. **[AVOID]** Depending on any foreign-hosted SaaS for storage of personal data, mirroring openDesk's logic — but note ZenDiS took ~€45M and years even with a dedicated agency; budget realistic multi-quarter timelines for a fully self-hosted stack, not a hackathon build.
8. **[ADAPT]** openDesk's component-stitching model (best-of-breed open-source parts under one identity/login) rather than either (a) building everything bespoke or (b) buying one closed monolith — cheaper to swap a failing part later.
9. **[ADOPT]** Organize navigation around what a specialist is doing this week (report status, request leave, find a document), not around org-chart/sub-department menus — the one clearly transferable lesson from Diia/Gosuslugi's life-event navigation.
10. **[AVOID]** Assuming a single powerful champion is enough political cover — 18F is proof that even a decade-old, widely-used, technically excellent unit can be deleted in one administrative action; get buy-in from the director *and* multiple sub-department heads, and make sure the platform's data/code are exportable regardless of who's currently sponsoring it.
11. **[ADAPT]** Jira's plugin-marketplace mechanism at small scale: design our data model so other departments can extend it (custom fields per sub-department, e.g., FinTech vs. Strategy needing different project metadata) without forking the core — this is how Jira colonized non-engineering teams without a rewrite.
12. **[ADOPT]** Free-at-point-of-use, no-procurement onboarding for any other department that wants to try the tool — mirror GOV.UK Notify's explicit "no monthly charge, no setup fee, no procurement process" as the internal cross-ministry pitch.
13. **[AVOID]** Publishing a beautiful component library as the primary early deliverable — Singapore's and GOV.UK's systems are the *visible tip* of years of platform/service work; a slick UI kit with no working shared service behind it will read as a poster, exactly the failure mode the reference-site audit already flagged.
14. **[ADOPT]** Log every access to sensitive/restricted employee fields (mirrors GDS "privacy by design" principle already in the reference prototype, and is standard practice everywhere data-localization law applies) — cheap to build now, expensive to retrofit.
15. **[ADAPT]** Slack/Asana's dogfooding-first launch sequence: ship to our own 23-person department first, get genuine daily use for 4-8 weeks, fix what's broken, *then* pitch to a second sub-department or ministry — do not demo to leadership before the builders themselves rely on it daily.

### C. Multi-tenancy as a technical pattern — GOV.UK Notify's actual mechanism, now documented

This was the most load-bearing gap given the project's explicit multi-tenant ambition, and it was also the most fully answerable one. GOV.UK Notify's own documentation makes the tenancy model explicit and concrete:

- **The "service" is the tenant boundary.** Every organisation that signs up to Notify creates a "service," and each service has its own isolated API keys, its own template library, its own recipient/contact data, its own settings, and its own daily sending allowance. An API key is scoped to exactly one service; **a call authenticated with one service's key cannot see or touch another service's templates, messages, or data** — there is no cross-tenant query surface exposed to API consumers at all. ([docs.notifications.service.gov.uk/rest-api.html](https://docs.notifications.service.gov.uk/rest-api.html))
- **Three key types map to a safe-by-default lifecycle, not just an access-control list**: a *test* key never actually delivers anything and never touches allowances (safe for CI/integration testing against a live-shaped API); a *team-and-guest-list* key (available pre-launch) can only deliver to verified team members plus up to five explicitly-added contacts, so a service in development literally cannot spam a real citizen by accident; a *live* key unlocks unrestricted sending only after the service passes GOV.UK's own approval process, and Notify's own guidance tells services to **rotate live keys regularly**. This is a "paved road" pattern: the tenant can't misconfigure their way into an incident during onboarding because the dangerous capability (send to anyone) is structurally unavailable until they're vetted.
- **Rate limits are enforced per-tenant, not globally**: 3,000 messages/minute per API key, and per-service daily caps (250,000 emails, 250,000 texts, 20,000 letters for live services; a much smaller 50-message combined cap for services still in trial). One noisy or compromised tenant cannot exhaust another tenant's throughput or allowance.

Translated to this project's multi-tenant ambition (one platform, eventually many ministries/departments): the transferable primitives are (1) a first-class "organisation"/"workspace" object that owns *all* other data — projects, people directory entries, activities — with every query implicitly scoped to it; (2) credentials/sessions that are scoped to one organisation and structurally cannot address another's records, rather than relying on row-level `WHERE tenant_id = ?` discipline alone; (3) a graduated trust lifecycle (sandbox/trial mode with hard caps → full production mode after an explicit approval step) for any *new* department or ministry onboarding, so a second ministry trying the tool cannot accidentally see or disrupt the first department's live data while they're still kicking the tires.

- **[ADOPT]** Model tenancy as Notify does: one top-level "organisation" entity that owns every other record, API/session tokens scoped to exactly one organisation, and no code path that can construct a cross-organisation query — enforce this at the data-access layer, not just in UI routing, from the very first schema design (retrofitting tenant isolation after the fact is materially harder than designing it in).
- **[ADOPT]** A graduated onboarding lifecycle for any new department/ministry — a bounded "trial" mode (their own data, hard caps on volume/seats, no cross-visibility to other tenants) before "live" mode — mirroring Notify's test/team/live key progression, so a second ministry can try the platform without any risk to the first department's data or an approval bottleneck blocking their first fifteen minutes of use.
- **[ADOPT]** Per-tenant rate/volume limits (not just per-user) on anything that could be abused or misconfigured (bulk notifications, exports, escalation broadcasts) — this is what actually stops one tenant's mistake from becoming every tenant's incident.

### F. Templates and zero-config defaults as a distinct adoption lever

GOV.UK Notify's template system is a clean, fully-documented example of this lever, separate from the "free and no procurement" adoption argument already in the base report. A Notify template is a reusable, personalisable message (email/SMS/letter) that a service defines once and then reuses indefinitely — "send the same thing to lots of people, as often as you need to, without writing a new message each time." Templates can be duplicated and organized into folders, meaning a *second* team standing up a new use case can clone a working template from another team rather than starting from a blank page. ([notifications.service.gov.uk/using-notify/templates](https://www.notifications.service.gov.uk/using-notify/templates))

The generalizable lever is: **the fastest path to a second team's adoption is not "the tool supports this use case" but "here is a working, pre-filled example of this exact use case you can clone and tweak in minutes."** A capability that technically exists but requires configuring from zero has a materially higher abandonment rate at the critical first-15-minutes moment than one that ships with a working starting point.

- **[ADOPT]** Ship every module (a project register, an escalation rule, an activity/RSVP event) with at least one pre-filled, realistic template a new sub-department or ministry can duplicate and edit, rather than an empty form with field labels — mirrors Notify's template-cloning pattern and is cheap to build once the first real instance exists.
- **[ADOPT]** When a second department wants to adopt a workflow that already works for the first (e.g., the weekly-comment-ritual project pattern), make "duplicate an existing configuration" a first-class, one-click action — not a rebuild-from-scratch process — the same mechanism that lets a Notify template spread from one team's email format to another's.

### H. Accessibility compliance regimes as concrete legal/procurement gates

The base report treated accessibility only as a Service Manual category. Three concrete regimes, each independently verified this session:

| Regime | Applies to | Standard referenced | Key dates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **WCAG** (W3C) | Voluntary global technical standard; referenced *by* the legal regimes below | 2.0 (Dec 2008) → 2.1 (June 2018) → **2.2 (Oct 2023, updated Dec 2024, current)**; each version is backward-compatible — meeting 2.2 automatically satisfies 2.1 and 2.0; three conformance levels A/AA/AAA, with **AA the de facto legal/procurement baseline** worldwide | 2.2 is current as of this research (Sept 2026) |
| **Section 508** (US Rehabilitation Act) | US federal agencies directly; extended to contractors/vendors via the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR), so any vendor selling ICT to a US federal agency must meet it | 2017 revised standards harmonized with WCAG 2.0 (conformance level not explicitly specified as A/AA in the fetched source — worth a targeted follow-up) | 2017 standards still in force |
| **EU Web Accessibility Directive** (2016/2102) | All EU member-state public sector websites and mobile apps | **EN 301 549**, which incorporates WCAG (public bodies conforming to EN 301 549 are presumed compliant) | New sites: Sept 23 2019; existing sites: Sept 23 2020; mobile apps: **June 23 2021**; member states must publish comparable monitoring methodology and submit compliance reports every 3 years from Dec 2021 |

Sources: [w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/), [section508.gov/manage/laws-and-policies](https://www.section508.gov/manage/laws-and-policies/), [EUR-Lex — Directive 2016/2102](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32016L2102).

The load-bearing pattern across all three: accessibility compliance is not a design preference anywhere in the developed-country reference set — it is a **procurement and legal gate with a named testable standard (WCAG, almost always at AA), a named enforcement mechanism, and a recurring reporting cadence.** Uzbekistan does not currently have an equivalent binding regime for this project (unverified, worth checking directly with the ministry's legal/procurement office), but if this platform is pitched to a second ministry or eventually a wider government rollout, "does it meet WCAG 2.2 AA" is the specific, checkable question a skeptical adopter elsewhere in government would eventually ask, and building to that bar now is far cheaper than retrofitting later.

- **[ADOPT]** Build to WCAG 2.2 AA from the start (color contrast, keyboard navigation, screen-reader labeling, focus order) — it is cheap when designed in from the beginning and the internationally recognized bar any future cross-ministry or donor-funded review would apply.
- **[ADOPT]** A one-page accessibility statement for the platform (what standard it targets, known gaps, a feedback channel) — mirrors the EU Directive's mandated accessibility-statement requirement and is a low-cost trust signal even where not legally mandated in Uzbekistan yet.

### I. Procurement/vendor mechanics: what "a second ministry adopts this" actually requires

- **[ADAPT]** Even without a formal marketplace, borrow G-Cloud's *effect*, not its bureaucracy: pre-package this platform as a describable, fixed "service" (what it does, what data it touches, what it costs to run, what support looks like) that a second ministry's own procurement/legal office can evaluate quickly, rather than requiring a bespoke technical evaluation each time — a one-page "service description" is the internal equivalent of a G-Cloud listing.
