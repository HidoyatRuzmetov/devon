# Portfolio management, status reporting, KPIs/OKRs, analytics dashboards

**TL;DR**
1. The best tools (Linear, Basecamp) win by shrinking status reporting to one number plus one sentence, on a fixed weekly cadence, never a free-form report — copy the ritual, not the tool.
2. RAG/traffic-light status is universally used and universally gamed under fear; the fix is separating **status** (fact: on-time/at-risk/blocked) from **confidence** (judgment) and auto-escalating on missed dates, not on self-reported color, exactly as our reference prototype's Friday ritual already implies.
3. OKRs fail in bureaucracies almost every time they're imposed top-down or tied to pay/performance review — for a 23-person department we should use lightweight quarterly objectives with 2–4 key results per sub-department, never individual OKRs, and never link them to appraisals.
4. Executives read one page, not a dashboard: a single "department one-pager" (status, blockers, decisions needed) beats any BI tool; Grafana/Metabase-style dashboards are for the analyst layer, not the director's Monday morning.
5. Government delivery units that worked (UK IPA/GMPP, Malaysia PEMANDU) succeeded via a small independent unit + monthly review cadence + public/semi-public dashboards; they failed or shrank when ratings became political ammunition — we should route escalation to help, not to blame, from day one.

## 1. Status-report cadences: what the tools that don't feel like Jira actually do

**Linear.** Linear organizes work into **Cycles** (fixed-length sprints, typically 1–2 weeks) and **Projects**, which sit inside **Initiatives** (Linear renamed "Roadmaps" to "Initiatives," confirming the industry consensus that a roadmap is really a grouped set of projects with a shared outcome, not a Gantt chart) [Linear docs]. Every Project carries a **Health** field — On Track / At Risk / Off Track — plus a free-text **Project Update** that the owner posts on a cadence (commonly weekly). The core UX bet is that a status update is a *forced, tiny, structured artifact* attached to the object itself (not a separate email or meeting), and prior updates are kept as a timeline so "how did this drift" is answered by scrolling, not asking. Linear's public "Linear Method" material frames this as replacing status meetings with async, tool-native updates and treating the tool as the source of truth rather than a reporting layer bolted onto real work [linear.app/method].

**Basecamp — Hill Charts.** Basecamp's Hill Chart is the single most-copied idea in this space and deserves the most attention because it solves the real problem: binary/percentage status ("60% done") lies about the thing that actually matters, which is *whether the unknowns are resolved*. The hill has two slopes: the **uphill** side means "figuring out what to do" (unknowns, exploration, risk), the downhill side means "getting it done" (known, mechanical execution) [basecamp.com/shapeup]. Team members drag colored dots (representing sub-scopes of a project) into position on the curve; Basecamp logs every move so a manager can compare snapshots over time and see **movement**, not just position — a dot that hasn't moved in two weeks is "effectively a raised hand" without anyone having to say "I'm stuck" out loud [basecamp.com/shapeup]. The documented failure mode: if a scope is too coarse (bundles several independent problems), the dot gets stuck because it's actually three dots pretending to be one — the fix Basecamp documents is forcing scopes small enough that a single dot can move continuously. This is a strong, importable idea: **progress is a position + a trend, not a percentage.**

**Range and Standuply** occupy the "async standup" niche — a daily/weekly prompt ("what did you do, what's next, any blockers") posted into Slack/Teams, aggregated into a digest. They reduce meeting time but produce narrative text that nobody reads at scale past ~15 people; multiple practitioner complaints (visible across G2/Reddit-style discussion, consistent with the category's general reputation) center on "standup fatigue" — filling the form becomes theater once nobody visibly acts on it. The lesson isn't "build a standup bot," it's "don't build a text-collection ritual without a visible consumption ritual on the other end" (someone has to read it and act, publicly, on a fixed day).

**Height** (largely folded into Notion's AI-project-management push after its 2024 acquisition) tried "autopilot" status: an AI infers project health from underlying task completion/velocity rather than asking a human to self-report. This is the frontier worth watching but not worth building ourselves yet — inferred status needs a lot of clean underlying task data (due dates, real completion signals) that a 23-person department starting from zero won't have for 1–2 quarters.

## 2. Portfolio health scoring: RAG, confidence, and why self-reported color always drifts green

RAG (Red/Amber/Green) is close to universal in government and enterprise PMOs, and equally universally criticized. The failure mode is consistent across every source we could examine: reported status trends optimistic ("green until it's suddenly a crisis") because red carries a **social cost** to the reporter, not an operational one to the org. The UK's Infrastructure and Projects Authority (IPA, merged into the new National Infrastructure and Service Transformation Authority, NISTA, in April 2025) ran the **Government Major Projects Portfolio (GMPP)** — the UK's central register of its largest projects (rail, schools, hospitals, defence, IT, transformation programmes), reviewed centrally with a small (~180-person) unit reporting into the Cabinet Office and HM Treasury [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrastructure_and_Projects_Authority]. Its own 2014 annual report is unusually candid: it attributed persistent delivery failure to "lack of project management skills across the Civil Service; too fast a turnover of project leaders; inadequate assurance processes and institutional knowledge" — i.e., the failure wasn't the rating scale, it was capability and continuity underneath it [same source]. The IPA's actual practice used a five-point **Delivery Confidence Assessment** (Red / Amber-Red / Amber / Amber-Green / Green) rather than plain three-point RAG specifically because three buckets compress too much signal — "Amber" became a dumping ground covering everything from "minor risk" to "about to blow up," which is the single most common complaint about RAG in practice.

**The model worth adopting:** separate two axes that RAG conflates:
- **Status** (objective, derivable from data): On Track / At Risk / Blocked / Slipped, computed primarily from *dates vs. today* and *dependency state*, not typed by a human.
- **Confidence** (subjective, owner-declared): a short, mandatory one-line reason whenever status is not On Track, entered as text, not a color the owner can silently pick.

This mirrors our reference prototype's rule almost exactly: "a missed deadline or blocked dependency automatically moves the item into the management review queue" — i.e., **escalation is triggered by the calendar and the dependency graph, never by a human choosing to raise their hand.** This is the single best idea already in our own brief and it is validated by exactly what the IPA's own retrospective says is missing elsewhere (assurance that isn't just self-report).

## 3. Roadmaps: Linear Initiatives, Productboard, Aha!, Airfocus

| Tool | Unit of planning | Status model | Best fit | Known complaint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Linear Initiatives | Grouped Projects with milestones | Health per project rolls up to initiative | Engineering-adjacent orgs, fast-moving | Weak for non-software portfolios; opinionated data model resists customization |
| Productboard | Feature/insight-driven roadmap, customer feedback linked to features | Prioritization score (RICE/ICE-style), not a health field | Product teams justifying *why* something is on the roadmap | Heavy setup; steep learning curve for non-PMs; expensive per-seat |
| Aha! | Strategy → initiatives → releases → features, very structured hierarchy | Custom fields, extensive but complex | Enterprises wanting a full strategy-to-execution chain | Frequently cited as the most feature-bloated/complex in this category — the opposite of "no training needed" |
| Airfocus | Modular, prioritization-framework-first (drag scoring weights) | Configurable scorecards | Teams that want a lighter Aha! | Newer, smaller ecosystem; fewer integrations |

For a government department, all four are over-built: they assume a *product* organization justifying a backlog to stakeholders. Our actual need is closer to Linear's model (grouped initiatives with a rolled-up health signal) than to Productboard/Aha!'s prioritization-scoring machinery — a ministry department does not need RICE scores, it needs "what are our 3 quarterly priorities, what feeds into them, are we on pace."

## 4. OKRs: what breaks them, especially in bureaucracies

OKRs originated as Intel's "iMBOs" under Andy Grove in the 1970s (documented in *High Output Management*, 1983), reached Google via John Doerr around 1999, and spread through tech (LinkedIn, Twitter, Uber, GitLab) [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objectives_and_key_results]. The documented failure modes that survive contact with any organization, government or not:

- **Cascading OKRs top-down through every layer** turns them into a waterfall of assigned targets — the opposite of the bottom-up alignment they're supposed to create. Google's own early practice (per Rick Klau, 2017) explicitly *skipped* individual-level OKRs, especially for younger/smaller organizations, calling them "redundant" [same source].
- **Task-list conflation**: individual OKRs degrade into to-do lists, blurring goal-setting with performance review — the moment OKRs are scored and that score touches a bonus or performance rating, people set targets they know they'll hit (sandbagging) instead of ambitious ones, defeating the entire premise.
- **Government-specific pattern** (not found in a citable source during this research pass, flagged as practitioner consensus, not verified — see Open Questions): public-sector OKR/KPI programs tend to fail when metrics are imposed by a central reform unit with no operational control over the levers that would move them, and when the review cadence is annual (matching budget cycles) rather than quarterly — by the time a problem shows up in an annual KPI report it's a year old.

**Recommendation implied by this evidence:** run OKRs (or a renamed lighter equivalent — "quarterly priorities") at the **sub-department level only**, never per-individual, never scored against pay, reviewed quarterly not annually, with 3–5 objectives total across the whole 23-person department (not 3–5 per unit) so leadership can actually hold all of them in their head — which is itself the standard practitioner guidance from Lattice, Perdoo, Quantive (formerly Gtmhub) and Profit.co, all of whom converged on "3 objectives, 3-5 key results each, department-level not individual" as the default template in their onboarding flows as of the last training-data-verified state of those products (2024–2025); we could not re-verify current 2026 pricing/features for these four via live fetch in this pass (search budget was exhausted) — treat their specific claims as directional, not current fact, until verified separately.

## 5. KPI dashboards: what executives actually read

Two design authorities converge on the same point from different angles. **Edward Tufte**'s data-ink ratio says every visual element should carry information — no gridlines, shadows, or 3D effects that don't encode data — and his **small multiples** technique (many small aligned charts on a consistent grid, same scale, over time) is the correct way to let a director compare 4 sub-departments at a glance rather than reading four independent, differently-scaled charts [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Tufte]. **Geckoboard**'s applied guidance (their own published best-practice list) adds the operational rules that turn Tufte's theory into a buildable spec: define one purpose per dashboard before designing it; every metric must tie back to that purpose; round numbers (no false precision — "82%" not "81.7346%"); avoid pie/area charts for comparison; group related metrics with section titles; put the most important number top-left (where eyes land first); always show a metric next to its **target or historical comparison** so a bare number can be judged good or bad; and — critically — kill metrics that never change or that are too noisy to show a trend, because a dashboard tile that never moves trains people to stop looking at the whole dashboard [geckoboard.com/blog].

Applied to us: **Grafana** is the wrong tool (built for time-series infra/observability — Prometheus, InfluxDB — not business KPIs; even its own ecosystem treats "business dashboards" as a secondary use case). **Metabase** (open-source, self-hostable, SQL/GUI query builder) is the right shape *if* we ever need ad hoc analyst-style BI on top of our own database, and self-hosting matters directly for Uzbekistan's data-localization law. **Databox** and **Geckoboard** themselves are SaaS point solutions for wallboard-style KPI display, aimed at marketing/sales teams pulling from many external tools (Google Analytics, Salesforce, ad platforms) — not directly relevant to us since we won't be aggregating third-party SaaS metrics, but their *design conventions* (above) are exactly what we should copy for our own built-in dashboard, rather than adopting the tool.

**The strongest single finding of this whole section:** executives don't want a dashboard, they want a **one-pager**. Every source on "reporting up the hierarchy" (from the IPA's GMPP submissions up to Cabinet Office, to Basecamp's own internal practice, to standard COO/PMO practice) converges on a single-page-per-review-cycle artifact — status, top risks/blockers, decisions needed, next milestone — because a director reviewing 5 units' worth of work has minutes, not an analysis session. A rich interactive dashboard is for the person who *builds* the one-pager, not the person who reads it.

## 6. Dependency/risk registers, delay prediction, capacity views

**Risk/dependency registers** in mature PMO practice (UK Green Book / Orange Book risk-management guidance, standard PRINCE2/MoP practice) are simple structured logs: risk description, likelihood, impact, owner, mitigation, review date, linked project(s). The pattern worth copying is *linking* — a risk or a blocked dependency should be a first-class object that a project can point at, so "why is this at-risk" resolves to a specific named blocker with an owner, not a comment buried in a status update.

**Capacity/workload tools** (Float, Resource Guru, Asana's built-in Workload view) all converge on the same visualization: a person-by-week grid, color-coded by percentage of capacity allocated, so overallocation is visible as a color change rather than a number you have to compute. Asana's Workload sits inside the same tool people already use for tasks (lower friction, weaker at true resource-leveling); Float and Resource Guru are dedicated scheduling tools with drag-to-reassign UX, better for agencies staffing many concurrent client engagements, arguably overkill for a single 23-person department with ~14 projects. For us, a light version of the Asana pattern (a grid, not a dedicated tool) is enough at current scale, revisited only if/when the platform scales to multiple departments/ministries with real staffing conflicts across units.

## 7. Government PMO practice: what actually worked and what didn't

**Malaysia's PEMANDU** (Performance Management and Delivery Unit, est. 2009 under the Prime Minister's Department) ran Malaysia's National Key Result Areas (NKRAs) through an intensive **"lab" methodology**: 6–8 week structured workshops where cross-agency teams built detailed delivery plans with named KPIs, then tracked them via public "Government Transformation Programme" annual reports and dashboards. It is widely cited as one of the most influential government delivery-unit models globally, later exported as a consulting product to other governments. Its reputation dimmed after Malaysia's 2018 change of government, when PEMANDU was dissolved/restructured amid criticism that the transformation program had become tied to the previous administration's political narrative and that some KPIs had been selected for showcase value rather than genuine difficulty. **We could not verify current details of PEMANDU/its successor via live web fetch in this pass** — the specific post-2018 restructuring facts above are from general knowledge, not a source fetched in this session; flagged in Open Questions.

**UK IPA/GMPP**, detailed in Section 2, is the best-documented model of a small central unit (~180 people) running a portfolio-wide confidence rating across dozens of the government's largest projects, reporting to the Cabinet Office/Treasury, superseded by NISTA in April 2025 — a reorganization itself a signal that even a mature, decades-refined system keeps being restructured, meaning **no government portfolio-management model is "solved," they are all mid-iteration.**

**Indonesia, UAE, and Uzbekistan-specific systems**: we could not verify current details for Indonesia's SPBE (Sistem Pemerintahan Berbasis Elektronik) digital government maturity scoring, the UAE's Prime Minister's Office government performance dashboards, or any Uzbekistan ministry KPI system via live search or fetch in this research pass (search budget was exhausted mid-session and the pages fetched did not surface this content). ## 8. Anti-patterns, confirmed across every source touched

- **Vanity metrics**: a metric that "gives the rosiest picture possible" without being actionable — the standard example is page views for a company whose revenue doesn't depend on traffic, or headcount/user-acquisition numbers that look good while cost-per-acquisition quietly exceeds revenue per user [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanity_metric]. The general rule this implies for us: never put a metric on a department dashboard unless a specific person would change a specific decision if it moved.
- **Red-status fear**: consistently the reason RAG drifts green until the last responsible moment (Section 2) — the fix is structural (auto-computed status from dates/dependencies) not cultural (asking people to "be more honest").
- **Gaming via scope redefinition**: Basecamp's own documentation of Hill Charts shows the same dynamic in miniature — a stuck dot gets "solved" by quietly redefining the scope smaller, which is functionally identical to a project lead moving a KPI's target down instead of admitting the number is bad. Any scoring system needs an audit trail of scope/target changes, visible to reviewers, or this happens invisibly.
- **Dashboard tiles that never move**: Geckoboard's own guidance flags this as actively harmful — a static or unreadably-noisy metric trains viewers to stop looking at the whole board, not just that tile.

## Proposed portfolio/status model for us

**Objects**
- `Objective` (quarterly, department-wide, 3–5 total, owned by a sub-department head or the director)
- `Project` (owner = one named person, unit, target date, linked Objective, linked Risks/Dependencies)
- `StatusUpdate` (weekly, owner-authored, one-line comment + auto-computed status; append-only timeline, never overwritten)
- `Risk/Blocker` (description, owner, linked project(s), review date)
- `Milestone` (date-bound checkpoint inside a project, feeds the "upcoming deadlines" view)

**Fields on Project**: name, owner, unit, objective link, target date, computed status (On Track / At Risk / Blocked / Slipped — derived from today vs. target date and open blocking Risk objects, not human-typed), latest comment, comment history, % complete (optional, secondary to status).

**Rituals** (directly extending our reference prototype's existing rule):
- Weekly (Friday): every project owner posts one status comment. No comment by Friday EOD = automatically flagged "stale," itself a visible status, not silently ignored.
- Automatic escalation: target date passed without completion, OR a linked Risk marked "blocking" persists past its review date → project auto-moves into a `Management Review Queue`, visible to unit heads and the director, with no human required to raise it.
- Quarterly: sub-department heads set 2–4 Objectives with the director; reviewed, not scored against individuals, not linked to appraisal.

**Escalation path**: unit → sub-department head (weekly) → director (weekly, via the review queue only — directors should never have to read 14 individual status comments) → nothing above the director exists yet for us, but the object model should already support an eventual "ministry" tenant rolling up multiple departments' queues, since multi-tenancy is an explicit requirement.

**The three dashboards worth building first**
1. **Department Overview** (director's daily/weekly view): the four KPI-card pattern already in our reference prototype (workforce, active projects + on-track %, portfolio health trend, quarter's objectives with progress) plus the Management Review Queue front and center — this *is* the one-pager Section 5 says executives actually want, made live instead of static.
2. **Unit Board** (sub-department head's working view): a Linear-style project list scoped to one unit, status timeline per project, capacity grid (who's overloaded this month), and the unit's own Objectives progress.
3. **Portfolio Timeline** (cross-department, quarterly planning view): a small-multiples-style grid — one row per unit, one column per month — showing project bars and milestone markers, used only in the quarterly planning ritual, not a daily-use screen. This is the only place a Gantt-adjacent view belongs; it should not exist as a persistent daily nav item, because daily use of a timeline view is a documented power-user-only pattern that first-time civil servants won't parse in five seconds.

## What this means for us

1. **[ADOPT]** Auto-computed status from dates + dependency state, not human-typed color — kills RAG's core failure mode (green-until-crisis) documented at the IPA and implied by our own reference prototype's escalation rule.
2. **[ADOPT]** Weekly, forced, tiny status comments attached to the Project object itself (Linear/Basecamp pattern) instead of a separate "status report" document or meeting — five-second-readable, matches our zero-training mandate.
3. **[ADOPT]** Position + trend over percentage — borrow Basecamp's Hill Chart insight (is work moving, not just "how much is done") even if we don't build a literal hill widget; show status *history* per project, not just current state.
4. **[ADOPT]** One department-wide one-pager as the primary executive artifact; treat any richer dashboard as an analyst/PMO tool, not a director's default screen — every source on reporting-up-the-hierarchy agrees executives read a page, not a BI tool.
5. **[ADOPT]** Objectives at sub-department level only, 3–5 total across the whole department, quarterly cadence, never scored against individual pay or appraisal — this is the one thing every OKR failure-mode source agrees on and is cheap to get right from day one.
6. **[AVOID]** Individual-level OKRs/KPIs for 23 people — Google itself skipped these even at much larger scale ("redundant," per Rick Klau); for a government department they read as surveillance, not alignment, and will kill adoption instantly.
7. **[AVOID]** A dedicated roadmap tool (Productboard/Aha!/Airfocus) — their prioritization-scoring machinery (RICE, weighted scorecards) solves a "which of 200 feature requests do we build" problem we don't have; our roadmap is 3–5 objectives and ~14 projects, which is a rollup view, not a separate product.
8. **[AVOID]** Grafana-style time-series dashboards for department KPIs — wrong tool shape (built for infra metrics, not quarterly business/policy outcomes); if we ever need ad hoc BI, evaluate a self-hosted Metabase instance instead, which also satisfies data-localization by running on our own infrastructure.
9. **[ADAPT]** The five-point Delivery Confidence scale (Red/Amber-Red/Amber/Amber-Green/Green) the UK IPA uses — too granular for a 23-person department's daily use, but the *principle* (three buckets compress too much signal) argues for our On Track/At Risk/Blocked/Slipped four-state model rather than plain RAG's three.
10. **[ADAPT]** Async standup-style digests (Range/Standuply pattern) — useful as the *delivery mechanism* for the Friday status ritual (a Telegram bot that posts "3 projects need your comment by 5pm" and later "here's this week's department digest"), but only if someone visibly reads and acts on it publicly each week — otherwise it degrades into the same "standup fatigue" theater those tools are criticized for.
11. **[ADAPT]** Asana-style Workload grid for capacity — build a lightweight version (person × week, color by allocation %) inside our own tool rather than integrating a dedicated tool like Float/Resource Guru, which are overbuilt for a single department at current scale; revisit only once multi-tenant scale creates real cross-unit staffing conflicts.
12. **[ADOPT]** Every scope/target change gets an audit trail visible to reviewers — directly addresses the "quietly redefine the scope/target instead of admitting failure" gaming pattern documented in both Basecamp's own material and the vanity-metrics literature.
13. **[ADOPT]** No metric ships on any dashboard unless a named person can say which decision it changes — Geckoboard's own best-practice list and the vanity-metrics concept both converge here; this single rule prevents "portfolio health: 82%" from becoming decoration, which is the exact risk our reference prototype's KPI cards currently carry with zero underlying data model.
14. **[AVOID]** Treating a government delivery-unit brand (PEMANDU-style "transformation programme") as inherently durable — even the most internationally cited model was restructured after a political transition; build the *object model and rituals* to survive leadership change, not a program identity tied to one director's tenure.

### G1. Delay prediction, not just delay detection

The base report's "auto-computed status from dates vs. today" is delay **detection** (a fact check against the calendar), not delay **prediction** (an inference about the future from a trend). The brief asked for the latter. Three established approaches, assessed for fit:

- **Earned Value Management (EVM)** — the classical answer, built on three numbers per work item: Planned Value (PV, budgeted cost of work scheduled), Earned Value (EV, budgeted cost of work actually done), and Actual Cost (AC, real money/time spent). From these, Schedule Performance Index (SPI = EV/PV) and Cost Performance Index (CPI = EV/AC) forecast whether a project will finish late or over budget *before* the deadline arrives, and Estimate at Completion (EAC = Budget at Completion / CPI) projects a revised finish. [Wikipedia — Earned value management] confirms EVM's own documented limitation directly undercuts using it here: it "typically assumes that project accounting and project network schedule management are prerequisites," collecting "true and timely actual cost data" is "the most difficult aspect" of running it, and — worse for a trust-building first deployment — a 2019 study (Darling & Whitty, cited in the same source) found EVM is itself gameable: "sham progress reporting can emerge" when the people consuming the metric don't have the technical depth to catch a padded EV number. EVM needs task-level cost/effort accounting a 23-person department starting from zero will not have.
- **Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM)** — Goldratt's resource-constrained alternative to critical-path scheduling: instead of padding every task's own estimate, CCPM strips individual safety margins and pools them into shared **project and feeding buffers**, then the only number anyone watches is *buffer consumption rate* (are we burning our shared slack faster than the project is progressing). [Wikipedia — Critical chain project management] credits it with completing projects "10% to 50% faster and/or cheaper," but it demands resource-leveling calculations, dual (50%-confidence vs. safe) duration estimates per task, and — the article's own phrase — "organizational culture shifts," none of which a first-time civil servant will absorb in five seconds. This is a specialist PMO technique, not a department-wide UI pattern.
- **The heuristic actually worth building**: a **velocity-trend flag**, not a formal methodology — if a project has posted N consecutive weekly status comments without its stated percentage/milestone progress moving, or if the gap between "target date" and "today" is shrinking faster than the gap between "milestones completed" and "milestones remaining," flag it "at risk of missing target" *before* the target date passes, computed from the same StatusUpdate timeline the report's object model already proposes — no new data collection, no EVM accounting overhead, no CCPM buffer math. This is the same spirit as Basecamp's Hill Chart insight already adopted in Section 1 ("a dot that hasn't moved is a raised hand") extended from *is it moving* to *is it moving fast enough to land on time*.

**[ADOPT]** A simple trend heuristic on the existing StatusUpdate timeline (stalled progress + shrinking runway → auto-flag "at risk," before the date actually passes) — this is genuine delay *prediction*, cheap to compute, and needs zero new fields.
**[AVOID]** Full EVM for a 23-person department — the accounting overhead is disproportionate, and its own documented failure mode (gameable sham reporting under management pressure) is precisely the RAG-drift problem the rest of this report is designed to eliminate.
**[AVOID]** CCPM buffer management as a user-facing feature — valuable PMO theory, wrong altitude for a zero-training tool; the *principle* (watch the trend of shared slack, not individual task percentages) can inform the internal heuristic above without exposing buffer math to end users.

### G2. Telegram Bot API mechanics (verified against official docs + limits reference)

Telegram is named explicitly in the brief and is the dominant messenger in Uzbekistan, so the mechanics matter for real, not decoratively:

- **Message length**: `sendMessage` text is capped at **4,096 characters** for standard accounts, **8,192 characters** for Telegram Premium accounts [limits.tginfo.me]. This is generous for a one-line status ping or even a compact daily digest, but a full weekly department-wide report with 14 projects' comments would need to be split into multiple messages or (better) rendered as a link to the one-pager dashboard rather than pasted inline.
- **Sending frequency**: roughly **1 message/second** sustained to an individual chat (short bursts tolerated), **20 messages/minute** to the same group chat, and up to **30 messages/second** when broadcasting distinct messages across many different chats; the Bot API overall caps bots at **~30 requests/second** [limits.tginfo.me]. For a 23-person department this is nowhere near a constraint — it only matters if the platform later fans out to many departments/ministries broadcasting simultaneously, in which case a queued/throttled send worker (not naive per-user loops) becomes necessary at multi-tenant scale.
- **Formatting**: the Bot API supports rich text via `MessageEntity` objects and parse modes (Markdown/MarkdownV2/HTML) covering bold, italic, underline, strikethrough, spoiler, links, and code — enough to make a status digest visually scannable (bold project names, a status word, a link back to the dashboard) without needing images or custom UI.
- **Pinning**: `pinChatMessage` lets a bot pin a message in a chat; Telegram imposes **no cap on the number of pinned messages** in a group or channel [limits.tginfo.me], though in practice a channel/group with many pins loses the "this is the important one" signal pinning exists to provide — a UX discipline problem (pin one thing: this week's department digest, unpin last week's) not a platform limit.
- **Group vs. channel**: a **group** (or supergroup) is bidirectional — members can reply, which fits a sub-department's working chat where a Friday digest might trigger a quick thread; a **channel** is one-way broadcast from admins/bots to subscribers, which fits a department-wide or ministry-wide announcement feed where you want the digest seen but not talked over. The natural mapping: post the Unit Board's weekly digest into each sub-department's **group** (invites reply/discussion), and post the Department Overview one-pager into a leadership **channel** (broadcast only, pinned, no cross-talk).

**[ADOPT]** A Telegram bot that posts the Friday digest as a formatted (bold/linked) message into each unit's group and pins the current one-pager in a leadership channel, unpinning the prior week's — this uses exactly the platform's own affordances (formatting entities, pinning, group/channel distinction) rather than treating Telegram as a plain-text pipe.
**[ADOPT]** Keep bot-sent digests short (well under the 4,096-character cap) and link out to the live dashboard for detail — Telegram is the notification/summons layer, not the system of record.
**[AVOID]** Building any multi-tenant fan-out logic that assumes unlimited send rate — the ~1 msg/sec per-chat and ~30 msg/sec global caps are real ceilings once this scales beyond one department, and need a queue, not a naive loop, from the first multi-tenant deployment.

### G5. Colorblind-accessible status indicators

RAG/status color is the report's most recurent visual device, and the accessibility gap was real. [W3C WCAG 2.1, Success Criterion 1.4.1 "Use of Color"] states plainly: "Color is not used as the only visual means of conveying information" — and gives the directly analogous failure example of marking required form fields in red alone, which excludes users with color-vision deficiency, low vision, or monochrome displays. The same logic applies one-to-one to a red/amber/green status chip. WCAG's own recommended fixes, applied to this platform:

- **Redundant coding, not color alone**: pair every status color with a **shape and a text label** — e.g., a filled circle for On Track, a triangle for At Risk, a square for Blocked/Slipped, each with the word written out, not just tinted. This also directly serves the trilingual requirement (G6 below): the shape stays legible even where a translated label runs long or wraps.
- **Contrast, not just hue difference**: where color is used at all, WCAG's fallback guidance is that different **lightness** values (a 3:1 contrast ratio) can substitute for hue differentiation for users who can't perceive hue differences at all — meaning the three/four status states should differ in lightness/darkness, not only in which hue (red vs. green) they use, so a grayscale printout or projector still reads correctly.
- This is a **near-zero-cost fix** applied at design time (icon + label + text, chosen once, reused everywhere) versus a expensive retrofit once dozens of screens already hard-code bare color chips — it belongs in the design system before the first status chip ships, not after an accessibility audit finds it.

**[ADOPT]** Every status indicator in the platform (Project health, RAG-equivalent, Delivery Confidence, capacity-grid cells) ships as shape + icon + text label by default, color as reinforcement only — this is directly required by WCAG 1.4.1 and costs nothing if built in from the first component, unlike RAG-only competitors (most of the tools surveyed in this report, per their own screenshots, are color-only).

### G6. Multilingual (Uzbek/Russian/English) dashboard and label design

This project's core, explicitly-stated constraint (trilingual UI) was never touched in the base report's dashboard sections, despite directly affecting the "5-second readable one-pager" goal. Live fetches to Nielsen Norman Group's text-expansion guidance failed this pass (404s), so the following is flagged as general UX/localization knowledge, not freshly re-verified — but it is well-established, low-risk-of-being-wrong practitioner consensus, not a speculative claim:

- **Text expansion is real and asymmetric across our three languages**: Russian and other Cyrillic-script translations of short English UI labels commonly run 15–35% longer in character count than the English source (a widely cited localization-industry rule of thumb, most often quoted for German/Russian/Finnish); Uzbek in Latin script tends to run closer to English length but has longer average word length for some administrative/bureaucratic terms. The practical implication: any status chip, KPI card label, or nav item sized to fit English text *will* clip, wrap awkwardly, or overflow in Russian.
- **Design implications directly relevant to the "one-pager" and status-chip patterns already adopted in this report**:
  - Never hard-code fixed-width buttons/chips/labels sized to English text — use flexible-width containers, or design against the *longest* language (Russian) first, and let English/Uzbek be the languages with slack, not the other way around.
  - Prefer icon+short-label combinations (see G5's WCAG fix) over long text-only status words, precisely because an icon doesn't need translation or resizing at all — this recommendation reinforces G5's accessibility fix rather than competing with it; the two problems (colorblind users, multilingual text) share the same solution.
  - Numbers, dates, and percentages should use locale-aware formatting (decimal separators, date order) rather than a single hard-coded format across all three languages — a small detail that a first-time civil servant will notice immediately if wrong (e.g., date order confusion is a classic government-software complaint).
  - Test every dashboard/one-pager layout in Russian first, not last, since it's the language most likely to break a layout tuned to English string lengths; treat Uzbek Latin and English as the "does it still look this clean" check after Russian passes.

**[ADOPT]** Design every status chip, KPI card, and nav label against Russian string length as the worst case, not English — a "design for the longest language" rule costs nothing at the component-library stage and is expensive to retrofit once dozens of screens assume English-length strings.
**[ADOPT]** Icon+short-label status indicators (already required by G5's accessibility fix) as the default multilingual solution too — one design decision solves two of this report's identified gaps simultaneously.
**[AVOID]** Auto-translating one canonical English UI and shipping it through a translation layer without a dedicated pass checking layout breakage — this is the most common source of "obviously translated, not designed for us" government software, which directly undermines the "goes viral for UI/UX" ambition stated in this project's own brief.
