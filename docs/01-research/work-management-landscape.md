# Work-management product landscape

**TL;DR:** The category is bifurcating hard: opinionated, fast, keyboard-first tools (Linear) are winning developer/product-team love while all-in-one "do everything" tools (ClickUp, Monday, Jira) are absorbing complaints about bloat, notification noise, and admin-dependency — and 2026 is the year the market itself cracked, with Airtable sold for $1.3B (down from an $11B peak), monday.com losing 21% of its stock value and cutting 20% of staff on fears that agentic AI eats seat-based SaaS, ClickUp cutting 22% of staff, and Coda quietly dissolving into "Superhuman Docs." Jira remains the most-hated market leader precisely because its power comes from unbounded configurability (workflows, screens, permission schemes, custom fields) that nobody in a 23-person department should ever have to touch — Atlassian's own 2024–2026 response (new navigation, Jira Work Management convergence, Rovo AI) is an admission of this, not a fix. Basecamp is the sharpest counter-proof that constraints (flat pricing, no per-project custom fields, Hill Charts instead of Gantt/burndown) create calm at the cost of raw power. For a first-time-user, zero-training, Uzbek-government product, the winning move is Linear-style opinionated defaults + Basecamp-style hard constraints + a single flat-rate/tenant pricing model, not a feature-parity clone of any one competitor.

## 2. Why Jira is hated

Jira is the market leader (originally launched 2002, name derived from "Gojira"/Godzilla via the internal Bugzilla nickname — literally born as a monster joke [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jira_(software)]) and simultaneously the most consistently mocked tool in the category. The complaint pattern is remarkably stable across a decade of G2/Capterra reviews, Reddit threads (r/jira, r/projectmanagement, r/programming), and internal engineering-blog retrospectives, and it clusters into five repeatable failure modes:

1. **Configuration sprawl as a design principle, not an edge case.** Every Jira instance accretes its own workflow schemes, screen schemes, field configuration schemes, and permission schemes — and these are four *separate* objects a project can mix and match, multiplicatively. What starts as "just add a status" becomes an admin-only change request queued behind IT, because a workflow scheme is usually shared across multiple projects and editing it can silently break someone else's board.
2. **Permission schemes require a specialist.** Jira's permission model (global permissions × project permissions × issue-level security schemes × permission schemes bound to projects) is powerful enough to model a multinational's RBAC, which is exactly the problem for a 23-person department: nobody who isn't a certified "Jira admin" can safely add a field or change who sees what.
3. **Perceived slowness.** Board/backlog re-renders, JQL search latency, and plugin ("app") overhead are the most common performance complaints, especially in Data Center/self-hosted deployments — the opposite of Linear's sub-100ms design goal.
4. **Jargon that assumes Scrum fluency.** Epics, Stories, Sub-tasks, Story Points, Sprints, Boards vs. Backlogs, "swimlanes," "Components" vs. "Labels" vs. "Fix Versions" — a first-time civil servant has no prior mental model for any of these terms, and Jira does not explain them in-product.
5. **Admin dependency as an ongoing tax, not a one-time cost.** Because almost every meaningful change (new issue type, new field, new automation rule beyond the free tier) routes through a Jira admin, the tool creates a permanent bottleneck role — precisely the "administrator priesthood" model that a Uzbek ministry, which will not have a dedicated Jira admin FTE, cannot sustain.

**What Atlassian changed 2024–2026** (self-reported, i.e., an implicit admission of the above): Atlassian introduced a redesigned navigation ("simplified" left-nav, fewer clicks to core objects) and pushed **Jira Work Management** — a business-team edition using the same underlying platform but Trello-like boards/lists instead of Scrum ceremony — as the on-ramp for non-engineering teams, explicitly trying to peel Jira's "complexity for developers" reputation away from the base product. In parallel, Atlassian shipped **Rovo**, its enterprise AI layer (search across Jira/Confluence, AI agents for triage/summarization), positioned as a way to *paper over* configuration complexity with a conversational interface rather than removing the complexity itself. Rovo and JWM point at the same diagnosis: Atlassian knows the base product is not learnable in an afternoon, and its 2024–2026 strategy is "reduce clicks + add an AI translator," not "remove the sources of configuration debt." For a from-scratch Uzbek-government build, this is the single clearest lesson in the whole landscape: **do not build the permission/workflow/screen-scheme architecture that made Jira need an AI assistant to be usable.**

## 3. The pattern: "powerful yet simple" vs. "powerful and confusing"

Comparing Linear, Basecamp, and Things 3 against Jira and ClickUp surfaces a consistent structural difference, not just a taste difference:

| Dimension | Powerful-yet-simple (Linear / Basecamp / Things 3) | Powerful-and-confusing (Jira / ClickUp) |
|---|---|---|
| **Defaults** | One correct default per object (one workflow, one priority scale, one way to file work); customization is additive and capped | Every object is unconfigured-by-default and *expects* an admin to define it before first use |
| **Configuration surface exposed to end users** | Hidden or absent — Basecamp literally has no settings screen for most objects; Linear exposes team-level settings but not per-issue-type screen/workflow editing to normal users | Exposed everywhere, to everyone with admin rights, as a selling point ("infinitely customizable") |
| **Time-boxing as UX** | Linear's Cycles and Basecamp's 6-week Shape Up cycles force periodic reset and prevent infinite backlog sprawl — the calendar itself is the interaction design | No enforced rhythm; backlogs grow unbounded, "everything is customizable" includes whether there's a rhythm at all |
| **Status representation** | Hill Charts (uncertainty-first, not percent-complete) and Linear's Triage inbox (a queue, not a form) map to how work *actually* feels | Percent-complete progress bars and story-point burndown imply false precision and require training to interpret |
| **Speed as a design constraint** | Linear's offline-first local sync engine is explicitly engineered for perceived-instant interaction — speed is treated as a core feature, not an optimization afterthought | Jira Data Center and ClickUp with many custom fields are both commonly cited for lag; speed is not treated as a hard constraint anywhere in the roadmap |
| **What "power" means** | Power = doing the one right thing fast (keyboard-first, zero clicks to the next action) | Power = the number of things you *could* configure, whether or not anyone should |

The mechanism, stated plainly: **constraint is a feature.** Basecamp's flat pricing removes the temptation to gate features behind seats, which removes the temptation to add features just to make new tiers sellable. Linear's refusal to add a native Gantt chart removes an entire category of "should this project use Gantt or Kanban" bikeshedding. Things 3's single global inbox + areas + projects (no tags-vs-labels-vs-custom-fields proliferation) removes the "how should I categorize this" decision fatigue that Notion/ClickUp/Airtable all reintroduce by design. Every one of these products chose to be *unable* to do certain things, on purpose, and that inability is what makes them learnable in minutes.

## 4. Market-structure signals worth weighing heavily (2026 contrarian evidence)

- **Airtable sold to Bending Spoons for ~$1.3B in August 2026** after peaking at an $11B valuation in December 2021 and two rounds of layoffs (2022: 254 staff / ~20%; 2023: 237 staff) [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airtable]. A build-your-own-database, high-flexibility tool could not sustain a category-leading valuation once the AI-agent wave arrived.
- **monday.com's stock fell 21% in February 2026** on investor fear that "agentic AI tools" would disrupt seat-based SaaS work-management platforms, followed by a 20% workforce reduction in July 2026 tied to AI restructuring [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monday.com]. The market is actively pricing in the idea that a lot of what these tools sell (manual status updates, manual board configuration) will be automated away. **Correction/addition (gap-fill pass):** the Feb 2026 21% drop was not an isolated dip — by the July 2026 layoff, monday.com's stock was down roughly **50% from its earlier 2025 highs**, i.e., the AI-disruption fear compounded across the first half of 2026 rather than being priced in once and recovering. This makes the July restructuring read less like a discrete reaction to one bad month and more like a sustained re-rating of the whole seat-based-SaaS category.
- **ClickUp cut 22% of staff in May 2026**, on top of a 10% cut in 2023 [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ClickUp] — the "do everything" strategy has not converted into durable headcount-supporting growth.
- **Coda ceased to exist as an independent brand in 2026**, folded into "Superhuman Docs" under an AI-collaboration positioning [coda.io homepage, fetched Sept 2026]. The doc-database hybrid category (Coda, and by extension Notion/Fibery's model) is consolidating toward "AI writing surface," not surviving as a standalone PM category. **Correction (gap-fill pass):** the rebrand is confirmed but **not yet a clean cutover** — the fetched coda.io homepage itself states "Coda is now Superhuman Docs," but old "Coda" branding still persists in navigation, templates, and parts of the product during the transition period. Treat this as "rebrand announced and substantially executed," not "Coda brand fully retired," if citing this elsewhere.
- **Height**, a well-regarded fast/Linear-adjacent tool with an early "AI teammate," is reported to have pivoted its company focus toward general AI agents rather than continuing to invest in the PM product as such (unconfirmed live in this pass — flagged as an open question below).
- **Wrike has changed controlling owner three times in under three years** (Citrix → Vista Equity → Symphony Technology Group) [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrike] and holds an estimated 0.04% market share off ~2,710 customers — a reminder that "powerful, configurable, enterprise-friendly" positioning does not automatically produce durable market position.
- **Smartsheet itself only found product-market fit after deliberately deleting 60% of its own features in 2008–2010** because the fully-featured version was unusable [en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smartsheet] — the single most on-the-nose historical precedent in this research for "cut scope to gain adoption."

The throughline: 2026 is punishing complexity and per-seat pricing simultaneously, and rewarding either extreme simplicity (Basecamp's flat/calm model, still independent and profitable per 37signals' own public writing) or genuine AI-native reinvention — not the median "add more views and more integrations" strategy that defined 2015–2023 category growth.

## 5. What this means for us

1. **[ADOPT] Ship with almost no configuration screens visible to normal users.** One workflow per work-item type, one priority scale, one status set. Mirror Basecamp/Linear, not Jira's four-scheme model. Rationale: our users are civil servants, not IT admins, and we have no admin FTE to spend on scheme maintenance.
2. **[ADOPT] Time-box the work rhythm into the product itself** — the reference prototype's "Friday update + auto-escalation" ritual is already Linear-Cycle-shaped; formalize it as a first-class object (a weekly cycle per sub-department) rather than a comment convention. Rationale: enforced rhythm is what makes Linear and Shape Up feel calm instead of open-ended.
3. **[ADOPT] Use a Hill-Chart-style "where's the uncertainty" status instead of percent-complete bars** for at least the qualitative/strategy-heavy projects (EGDI audits, policy briefs) where a fake 62% is worse than "we haven't validated the approach yet." Rationale: Basecamp's own argument — percent-complete is dishonest on ambiguous work, which describes most of this department's output.
4. **[ADOPT] Flat, tenant-level pricing/quota model, not per-seat.** Even though this is internal software (no literal billing), design resource quotas (storage, automation runs) per *department/tenant*, not per person, so adding the 24th employee never feels like a cost decision. Rationale: Basecamp's flat-price philosophy avoids the "who gets a seat" politics that per-seat SaaS creates even internally.
5. **[ADOPT] Keyboard command palette (Cmd/Ctrl-K) from day one**, even in a first release aimed at non-technical users — make it optional and discoverable (a visible "⌘K search" affordance), not power-user-only. Rationale: it costs little to build early and is the single most-cited "why Linear feels fast" reason; retrofitting it later is much more expensive.
6. **[AVOID] Do not build a general-purpose custom-field/custom-workflow engine as a v1 feature**, however tempting for "future-proofing." Rationale: this is the exact mechanism (workflow schemes × screen schemes × permission schemes) that made Jira require a dedicated admin; every hour spent making status/fields "fully configurable per team" is an hour spent building the thing users hate about Jira.
7. **[AVOID] Do not adopt per-seat, per-tier feature-gating (e.g., "Automations only on Business tier").** Rationale: it's the ClickUp/monday.com pattern that produces confusing upsell friction and, per the 2026 layoffs at both companies, does not appear to be winning even commercially anymore.
8. **[ADAPT] Borrow Smartsheet's grid/spreadsheet mental model only for the specific views where our users already think in Excel** (budget tracking, KPI tables, headcount rosters) — not as the primary interaction model for everyday task work. Rationale: it lowers the learning curve for finance/HR-adjacent staff without becoming this dimension's version of Fibery's "you must design a schema first" tax.
9. **[ADAPT] Borrow Jira Work Management's core idea (one platform, one simplified UI layer for non-engineering business teams) but reject the "same underlying complex platform underneath" execution.** Rationale: the *intent* — give business users a Trello-simple surface — is right; Atlassian's mistake was leaving the powerful/complex object model reachable underneath, which leaks through the first time someone hits an edge case.
10. **[ADAPT] Adopt Asana's "views over one canonical data model" pattern (List/Board/Timeline are the same items rendered differently)** rather than ClickUp's "different view types with different capabilities" pattern, so switching a view never loses information or changes what a status means. Rationale: consistency across views is cheap to build correctly from day one and expensive to retrofit.
11. **[AVOID] Do not chase feature parity with ClickUp's "15+ views" or Airtable's "build your own app" flexibility.** Rationale: both companies are shedding staff in 2026 even as category leaders in "flexibility," which suggests flexibility alone is not what makes a tool go viral internally — legibility is.
12. **[ADOPT] Build the AI layer as an assistant that *reduces* clicks on the existing simple model (auto-drafting a weekly status comment, flagging a stalled project into the escalation queue), not as a Rovo-style "conversational workaround for underlying complexity."** Rationale: AI-as-crutch-for-complexity (Rovo) is a worse product decision than AI-as-accelerant-for-simplicity (a well-scoped assistant on top of Basecamp-simple objects); the former treats a symptom, the latter compounds an advantage.
13. **[AVOID] Do not use "Sprint/Epic/Story Point/Swimlane" Scrum jargon anywhere in the default UI**, since the department does policy/analytics work, not software sprints, and a first-time civil servant has zero prior exposure to that vocabulary. Use plain nouns already present in the reference prototype (Project, Update, Deadline, Owner).
14. **[ADOPT] Localization and Telegram-native notifications as parity features from v1, not an add-on** — none of the twelve tools researched treat Telegram as a first-class notification channel (all are Slack/email-first), which is a genuine differentiation opportunity for the Uzbek market where Telegram dominates messaging.
15. **[ADOPT] Plan for a Smartsheet-style compliance story (data localization, audit logging of who viewed/changed restricted HR fields) as a v1 architectural requirement, not a later add-on**, mirroring Smartsheet's FedRAMP/DISA IL-4 positioning as the credible template for "government-grade" trust — even though our deployment target (Uzbekistan on-prem/gov-cloud) is a different compliance regime, the pattern (named certifications + named public-sector references) is what buyers in this segment actually trust.
